What does your use cost?

In a week with 7 screen hours: screen and router 84 Wh, activities 278 Wh, most of it the coding hour.

In the same hour, the screen and router draw most of the energy. What the activity adds is small.

On a phone over Wi-Fi, chat draws about 15 Wh an hour and streaming video about 14 Wh; the router and screen account for 12 Wh of both (fp-001, fp-122, fp-130, fp-049, fp-126). The activity itself adds 0.8 to 3 Wh (fp-001, fp-017, fp-122, fp-130, fp-127, fp-049).

The limits matter. The router is charged by time; that rule reorders the two hours, so no ratio between them prints here. The phone's line is battery energy, about 6.2 Wh at the wall (lf-022); a television alone is 100 Wh or more. The chat row is the median text prompt, and the kind of chat moves the hour to 184 Wh (fp-025).

A chat prompt is small; a coding hour is not.

One median text prompt draws 0.3 Wh in the data center (0.24 to 0.6; fp-001, fp-017) and about $0.004 at list (px-003, px-004). An hour of agentic coding at the model's mean turns draws about 211 Wh and costs $1.01 at list (54 to 878 Wh; au-043, au-038, px-016); it is the one row where the server passes the device.

A median prompt times a count is an illustration, not a bound; the mean-class prompt is 0.34 Wh (fp-012). The energy of a cache read is an assumption with no measurement behind it. In this model a coding session's data-center energy runs from 30 to 2,900 median text prompts across the model's input extremes (mv-004); that is a modeled spread, not a measured range of sessions, so no single multiple holds.

Your use counts time once, so adding an activity never adds a second screen.

The screen and router sit in one row, charged by your screen hours. Each activity carries only its own data center and network draw: the count times the per-unit coefficient. At the starting fields, each activity equals the model's hour row less the shared screen and router.

Price stays in three lines and never one sum: what the work costs at API list, what platforms earn in those hours, which is revenue and not a cost, and seat and storage list prices. CO2e is the sum across layers at the grids named. Water stays by basin, and no water total prints anywhere.

How AI differs from data centers, in plain words.

A data center is a building; AI is a kind of work. Most of the electricity in data centers today runs search, video, storage, email, and business software, not AI; most of the growth now being built is for AI. The AI a person uses in a chat or streaming hour is a small part of that hour on their own screen and router; the coding hour is the exception, where the server passes the device. It can run in a hyperscale building, a regional colocation, a community node, a laptop, or a phone.

The building decides the water and the grid. The busiest building has the lowest energy per answer at equal model size, because its hardware waits idle least; that is arithmetic about utilization, not a reason to host one. A proposed data center is a question of megawatts, load factor, cooling, and the feed it draws, entered under A proposal. Hosting one does not supply a Nation's own computing, and one person's chat does not explain the buildout.

Five separations the page keeps, with the figures on each.
  • In the same hour the screen and router are about 12 Wh on every phone row with the router charged by time (a television alone is 100 Wh or more), and the activity adds 0.8 to 3 Wh, chat or video.
  • Most data center electricity is not AI today, on two definitions that must not be mixed: AI-focused facilities were 155 of 485 TWh worldwide in 2025 (32 percent, lf-001, IEA) and AI workloads 15 to 25 percent (lf-002, second-hand). The growth is AI: AI-focused facilities are 310 of the 460 TWh the IEA adds by 2030 (67 percent, lf-001), and in LBNL's US reference case AI servers reach 55 percent of data center energy in 2030 (lf-003); training is 20 to 35 percent of AI server draw (lf-004).
  • AI can run outside a data center: the place profile moves 1,000 prompts from 300 Wh in a hyperscaler to 24 Wh on laptops that are on anyway, at a cost in quality, and 11 to 29 percent of queries go to the cloud regardless (sf-035). A busy data center has the lowest energy per answer at equal model size because its hardware sits idle least; that is arithmetic about utilization, not a reason to site one, and a dedicated local node catches up only above about 2.8 million prompts a year on one grid.
  • Data centers run things that are not AI: the video, storage, office, and search rows.
  • One person's chat is not the buildout: ChatGPT's consumer-plan messages come to about 0.27 TWh a year at 0.30 Wh a text prompt (0.22 to 0.55), an illustration for one product, not a bound, against the IEA's 485 TWh global total. A proposed building is a question for the project scale panel and the Questions for Tribal Leaders, and hosting a building does not supply a Nation's own computing: those are separate decisions.
Per-task efficiency is not total demand.

Energy per AI task fell at least 10 times a year (ec-057; Google 33 times, fp-009) while Google's electricity rose 37 percent in 2025 (rs-003). Across Google, Microsoft, and Meta, electricity grew about 22 percent a year whatever revenue did (rs-001, rs-020, rs-033, fin-028, fin-029, fin-031). That is a dated description of three firms, not an elasticity or a forecast.

What wins, what loses, and what reverses it

Wins, at the default settings

License price (0); working without broadband (24 percent on Tribal lands lack it, sf-056); custody; carbon only as a declared clean feed, a scenario (9.1 kg a year on hydro; on one grid 125 kg against the cloud's 32 kg, gf-035, gf-001), with the local embodied line (130 kg a year, 54 at the low drive figure) printed beside on the local side only.

Loses, at the default settings

Energy: 434 against 110 kWh a year on the data center alone and 260 with the cloud's sync watts. Against the 260, the two tie at 4.3 W of sync draw (the input runs 1 to 10 W), at 5.7 kWh a seat-year for the cloud seat (2.2 to 6.6), or at a 27 W server, which is below the server's 30 to 60 W range, each with the other inputs at their defaults; so the page prints the range and never a ratio. Price whenever hours are paid: $10,616 against $9,840 at 60 dollars an hour ($4,736 at 25; $536 unpaid), with 2 assumed cloud admin hours a month against 14 local (labor was the largest line in Schleswig-Holstein, sf-003, sf-004, lf-057). A local open-source suite on one 45 W box is about $10,600 at 14 paid hours a month and $60 an hour, $4,740 at $25, and $536 if staff time is already paid (px-045, sv-042, sf-051, gf-054). Its energy is 263 to 788 kWh against the cloud's 110 kWh, a 2011 whole-service figure (sf-016).

The cloud figure is email, not a suite, and illustrative, so the page prints the spread and never the ratio. Compatibility and support are real costs (lf-056, sf-057, sf-006).

A local box wins on the link and on shared viewing, not on watt-hours.

On the same network the local box uses more energy per view than the platform at the measured intensity, and at these settings no audience size closes the gap; one change to the inputs puts the crossover at 170,000 to 310,000 views a year. Its idle floor is most of each view at low counts. It wins when the link out is slow, metered, or down, and when many people watch the same thing (sf-030, sf-031). An embedded cache wins on energy and loses on control, because the platform places it and decides what it holds (sf-029, lf-034).

Sharing data across Nations saves bytes, dependency, and consent, not kilowatt-hours.

Ten Nations each holding their own terabyte on boxes that are on anyway use about 65 kWh a year against 188 kWh for one central three-copy cloud copy (ec-021, lf-027). On whole boxes it is 4,430 kWh, and the only measured federation saving is bytes: 90 percent duplicate removal (sf-032) and PeerTube bandwidth (sf-031).

The central copy on R2 or B2 is 3.4 and 5.4 times cheaper than the federated set, most of whose price is ten operators' paid hours (px-050, px-052). "Already on" is a declared setting, one copy is not three, and ten nodes must be up.

Local keeps the data under the Nation's own control, which no row prices; a laptop already on uses less energy per answer, a dedicated node more at the counts shown, and a clean feed is an arrangement, not an architecture.

A dedicated community node for AI uses more energy per answer than a hyperscaler until it serves about 2.8 million prompts a year (0.88 to 17 million, or never), because a 40 W box idles all year (au-027, gf-054, sf-036). On one grid the carbon crossover is the same count. Laptops that are on anyway add 24 Wh per 1,000 prompts, 110 once the 29 percent of queries a small model cannot answer go to the cloud (sf-037, sf-035). A small model is a different product from a frontier one (lf-013), so quality is not equal and nothing ranks across model classes.

Carbon is compared on one grid. A node on a declared hydro feed (life-cycle 21 g, gf-035) against a hyperscaler on the US grid (350 g, gf-007) crosses at about 80,000 prompts a year, but only if the node is physically behind that meter and the other side is not, and hydro already committed elsewhere is not clean new supply. It is a scenario, never the default, and water on the reservoir convention rises when carbon falls (gf-040, gf-039).

At 10 TB, local still loses on watt-hours. It can win on price, within a limit, and the bill for leaving is mostly hours.

The cloud's figure counts drives only, in three replicas; the box's counts the whole box all year, and the model counts one four-bay box per copy at any size. At the illustrative 10 TB, a year on one box is about 443 kWh against the cloud's 188, and 1,330 at three copies, the count the cloud row assumes (lf-032, ec-021); copy count is not durability, and no failure, repair, or integrity model is in this record. At that size no single change to the inputs reverses the three-copy row, even with the nodes already on (225 kWh; ec-021, sf-044), but the size itself does, near 71 TB, because the cloud's drives-only figure grows with the data and a box's does not (lf-032, ec-021). A two-bay box at 15 W (sf-043), a node already on, or a size above about 24 TB reverses the one-copy row, where one box is held against three replicas; at 10 TB the two tie at 19.5 W (lf-032, ec-021). On one grid, carbon follows energy: 128 kg against 54 kg (gf-001).

Each price counts the store's list, or the box over its life and its power, plus 1 assumed paid hour a month. One box costs less a year than S3, R2, and B2 at list: $902 against $3,480, $2,520, and $1,570, each side with 1 assumed paid hour a month at $60 (sf-052, px-048, px-050, px-052, sf-051). At 3.5 paid hours a month, the top of a seller's figure (sv-042), the order reverses against R2 and B2, and at the same assumed copy count only S3 stays beaten. The paid hours that sink the office suite and the shared data come from 14 hours a month and 10 operators (sv-042, sf-051), not from one Nation's one box.

The boundary leans toward the cloud: its figure is drives only and the box is whole. The one whole-service figure in the record (ec-026, another boundary) makes this data 1,660 to 2,800 kg a year, a reading on a wider boundary beside the cloud's 66 kg drives-only floor: four data centers in one agency's study, not a bound on any one service. The record holds no whole-service figure for a local box, so the two are never subtracted, and the watt-hour order is a finding on the record's one shared boundary, not on every boundary.

At the assumed hours the bill for leaving is mostly people's time: $2,400 of the $3,300 from S3 (px-049, sf-051), and those hours have no source. Egress is the smaller part, $900, and the vendors' exit programs can take it to zero on their terms (mv-001, mv-002, mv-003). The wire takes at least 9.3 days at 100 Mbps (sf-056).

None of this prices who holds the data. That question stays with each Nation.

What actually saves energy?

Pool demand (batching, federation of users) saves the most per unit at these settings: 4,010 Wh before, 546 Wh after, 14 percent of before.

A per-unit saving is not a total one.

Rebound is outside the unit: per-task energy falls 10 times a year (ec-057; Google 33 times, fp-009) while totals rise (Google plus 37 percent, rs-003), price elasticity sits near one at the provider level (ec-044), and the IEA says so (ec-058). The page prints the per-unit number and the count beside it, and this pair as the reason a per-unit saving is not a total one.

Prompts an hour

No source measures a chat hour. Kind of chat

The text figures stop at 0.60, below GPT-5 minimal reasoning (0.67), Claude 3.7 (0.95) and o3 (1.18) in the same preprint. medium_prompt is Jegham's GPT-4o medium query (fp-021); reasoning is Joule's query 15 times longer than typical (fp-019); high_reasoning is Jegham's GPT-5 high reasoning, medium query (fp-025). Jegham is a preprint that Joule says overstates production figures by 4 to 20 times (fp-020). Grids

Data center grid

Carbon is location-based only. Average, short-run margin, and long-run margin differ nine times for the same kilowatt-hour in the Northwest, so the choice is printed on the figure. Hydro, wind, and solar are life-cycle factors (gf-035, gf-034, gf-033) while eGRID rates count combustion only, and a feed is a declared supply arrangement, not a location factor, unless the machine is physically behind that meter; hydro already committed elsewhere is not clean new supply. Wind, solar, and diesel print no generation water until a claim for them is added. Washington's in-state rate is CO2, not CO2e, and includes exports. The BPA factor is regulator-assigned for power bought from BPA, on a different accounting method from eGRID, so the gap between the two is not a grid effect.
