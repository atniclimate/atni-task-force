In the same hour, the screen and router draw most of the energy. What the activity adds is small.

On a phone over Wi-Fi, chat draws about 15 Wh an hour and streaming video about 14 Wh; the router and screen account for 12 Wh of both. The activity itself adds 0.8 to 3.0 Wh (fp-001, fp-017, fp-122, fp-130, fp-127, fp-049).

The limits matter. The router is charged by time; that rule reorders the two hours, so no ratio between them prints here. The phone's line is battery energy, about 6.2 Wh at the wall (lf-022); a television alone is 100 Wh or more. The chat row is the median text prompt, and the kind of chat moves the hour to 184 Wh.

A chat prompt is small; a coding hour is not.

One median text prompt draws 0.3 Wh in the data center (0.24 to 0.6; fp-001, fp-017) and about $0.004 at list (px-003, px-004). An hour of agentic coding at the model's mean turns draws about 211 Wh and costs $1.01 at list (54 to 878 Wh; au-043, au-038, px-016); it is the one row where the server passes the device.

Medians are a floor for totals; the mean-class prompt is 0.34 Wh. The energy of a cache read is an assumption with no measurement behind it. A session is anywhere from 30 to 2,900 prompts, so no single multiple holds.

Your Use counts time once, so adding an activity never adds a second screen.

The screen and router sit in one row, charged by your screen hours. Each activity carries only its own data center and network draw: the count times the per-unit coefficient. At the starting fields, each activity equals the model's hour row less the shared screen and router.

Price stays in three lines and never one sum: what the work costs at API list, what platforms earn in those hours, which is revenue and not a cost, and seat and storage list prices. CO2e is the sum across layers at the grids named. Water stays by basin, and no water total prints anywhere.

AI Is Not the Building

How AI differs from data centers, in plain words.

A data center is a building; AI is a kind of work. Most of the electricity in data centers today runs search, video, storage, email, and business software, not AI; most of the growth now being built is for AI. The AI a person uses in a chat or streaming hour is a small part of that hour on their own screen and router; the coding hour is the exception, where the server passes the device. It can run in a hyperscale building, a regional colocation, a community node, a laptop, or a phone.

The building decides the water and the grid. The busiest building has the lowest energy per answer at equal model size, because its hardware waits idle least; that is arithmetic about utilization, not a reason to host one. A proposed data center is a question of megawatts, load factor, cooling, and the feed it draws, entered with no defaults under Project Scale. Hosting one does not supply a Nation's own computing, and one person's chat does not explain the buildout.

Five separations the page keeps, with the figures on each.
  • In the same hour the screen and router are about 12 Wh on every phone row with the router charged by time (a television alone is 100 Wh or more), and the activity adds 0.8 to 3 Wh, chat or video.
  • Most data center electricity is not AI today, on two definitions that must not be mixed: AI-focused facilities were 155 of 485 TWh worldwide in 2025 (32 percent, lf-001, IEA) and AI workloads 15 to 25 percent (lf-002, second-hand). The growth is AI: AI-focused facilities are 310 of the 460 TWh the IEA adds by 2030 (67 percent, lf-001), and in LBNL's US reference case AI servers reach 55 percent of data center energy in 2030 (lf-003); training is 20 to 35 percent of AI server draw (lf-004).
  • AI can run outside a data center: the place profile moves 1,000 prompts from 300 Wh in a hyperscaler to 24 Wh on laptops that are on anyway, at a cost in quality, and 11 to 29 percent of queries go to the cloud regardless (sf-035). A busy data center has the lowest energy per answer at equal model size because its hardware sits idle least; that is arithmetic about utilization, not a reason to site one, and a dedicated local node catches up only above about 2.8 million prompts a year on one grid.
  • Data centers run things that are not AI: the video, storage, office, and search rows.
  • One person's chat is not the buildout: all of ChatGPT's text chat is about 0.27 TWh a year on Google's median prompt (0.22 to 0.55 across the median figures), text only, a floor for one product against the IEA's 485 TWh global total. A proposed building is a question for the project scale panel and the Questions for Tribal Leaders, and hosting a building does not supply a Nation's own computing: those are separate decisions.
Per-task efficiency is not total demand.

Energy per AI task fell at least 10 times a year (ec-057; Google 33 times, fp-009) while Google's electricity rose 37 percent in 2025 (rs-003). Across Google, Microsoft, and Meta, electricity grew about 22 percent a year whatever revenue did (rs-001, rs-020, rs-033, fin-028, fin-029, fin-031). That is a dated description of three firms, not an elasticity or a forecast.

Architectures

An office suite on a local server costs hours, not licenses.

For 50 people, cloud seats are about $9,840 a year with 2 assumed admin hours a month. A local open-source suite on one 45 W box is about $10,600 at 14 paid hours a month and $60 an hour, $4,740 at $25, and $536 if staff time is already paid (px-045, sv-042, sf-051, gf-054). Its energy is 263 to 788 kWh against the cloud's 110 kWh, a 2011 whole-service figure (sf-016).

The cloud figure is email, not a suite, and illustrative, so the page prints the spread and never the ratio. Compatibility and support are real costs (lf-056, sf-057, sf-006).

A local box wins on the link and on shared viewing, not on watt-hours.

On the same network the local box never beats the platform's serving energy at the measured intensity. Its idle floor is most of each view at low counts. It wins when the link out is slow, metered, or down, and when many people watch the same thing (sf-030, sf-031). An embedded cache wins on energy and loses on control, because the platform places it and decides what it holds (sf-029, lf-034).

Sharing data across Nations saves bytes, dependency, and consent, not kilowatt-hours.

Ten Nations each holding their own terabyte on boxes that are on anyway use about 65 kWh a year against 188 kWh for one central three-copy cloud copy (ec-021, lf-027). On whole boxes it is 4,430 kWh, and the only measured federation saving is bytes: 90 percent duplicate removal (sf-032) and PeerTube bandwidth (sf-031).

The central copy on R2 or B2 is 3.4 and 5.5 times cheaper than ten operators' hours (px-050, px-052). "Already on" is a declared setting, one copy is not three, and ten nodes must be up.

Local wins on custody, not on watt-hours; a clean feed is an arrangement, not an architecture.

A dedicated community node for AI uses more energy per answer than a hyperscaler until it serves about 2.8 million prompts a year (0.88 to 17 million, or never), because a 40 W box idles all year (au-027, gf-054, sf-036). On one grid the carbon crossover is the same count. Laptops that are on anyway add 24 Wh per 1,000 prompts, 110 once the 29 percent of queries a small model cannot answer go to the cloud (sf-037, sf-035). A small model is a different product from a frontier one (lf-013), so quality is not equal and nothing ranks across model classes.

Carbon is compared on one grid. A node on a declared hydro feed (life-cycle 21 g, gf-035) against a hyperscaler on the US grid (350 g, gf-007) crosses at about 80,000 prompts a year, but only if the node is physically behind that meter and the other side is not, and hydro already committed elsewhere is not clean new supply. It is a scenario, never the default, and water on the reservoir convention rises when carbon falls (gf-040, gf-039).

Method

What This Is

These are modeled estimates, not measurements: a bottom-up process model, units of work times published coefficients, checked against published figures. It is not an econometric model; it estimates no coefficient, and its ranges are spreads, not distributions. Each row is units of work times published coefficients, plus the share of hardware that sits idle to serve it, scaled by the building and the grid where it runs. No vendor has published a measured production figure for most rows, no Tribally owned facility in the record has published its electricity use or total water, and the back-test printed here shows where the model lands within a factor of two and where it misses by five to eight. Change a setting and the answer changes; that is the point of the tool. It says nothing about any site, stream, rate class, or Treaty-reserved right, and it never turns sovereignty, consent, or resilience into a number.

The model counts what one unit of use needs: its own energy, plus its share of the hardware that waits idle to serve it, on every machine it touches: data center or local node, network, home router, and device. Where it runs sets the carbon and the water; both sides are compared on one grid. Who bills it sets the price, and the page names which kind of price it is: a list price, a seat price, a self-run cost, or what a platform earns, which is revenue, not a cost. Each number is a published coefficient times a count, never a meter reading, and each carries its spread, its sources, and the one input that moves it most. The idle term that decides where something should run has no published measurement behind it yet.

Calibration

The idle fraction is an accelerator's 0.11 (ec-020), because every row the multiplier touches is accelerator work. The conventional-server anchors solve to 0.25 (sf-008) and state 0.36 (sv-039); they print here and set no row. The idle term is untested: no back-test row touches it.

Measure It Yourself

Each instrument below replaces an assumption with a field measurement, and says what a finding would change.

  • Smart plug on the box. Any metering plug with logging: one week of idle watts and one week with the service in use, for the office server, the media box, the NAS, or the AI node. It replaces office_w, mini_w, nas_w, node_w, and the room PUE assumption. A measured idle under twenty watts halves every local energy row; a measured idle over a hundred watts doubles them.
  • GPU and host meters on the AI node. nvidia-smi --query-gpu=power.draw --format=csv -l 1 on a Linux or Windows node, or sudo powermetrics --samplers cpu_power,gpu_power on a Mac, logged with the token count of each answer, gives joules per token at the wall rather than GPU only. It replaces j_node and tests the idle multiplier against a real load curve: power at ten, thirty, fifty, and one hundred percent of the node's throughput gives the idle fraction directly, the term no published figure covers. A plug-meter reading of a server at several loads, or a published SPECpower curve for the box, does the same for the place multipliers.
  • Packet capture on the router. The router's own traffic counters, or a mirror port: gigabytes per hour of chat, video, short video, and the agent session, by app. It replaces kb_prompt, coding_gb_per_hour, and the video gigabytes per hour. A measured agent session above 0.5 GB an hour moves the network layer onto the coding row.
  • Billing and token logs. The API console's monthly tokens by input, cached, and output, and the invoice; the seat invoice; the storage and egress invoice. They give the real cache-read share c (today an assumption, 0.01 to 0.25), the real turns per hour, and the real dollars per hour of agent time, the one back-test the model misses by about three times. A logged cache share near 0.25 doubles the coding rows' energy.
  • Utility bills and the Nation's own rate. The tariff, the delivered mix, and, if the Nation runs a facility, its PUE, electricity, and water. They replace tariff, the hydro share, and the place profile. The first Tribally operated figures in the record would replace the "no published figure" sentence.

What would change the model itself: a measured production figure for any catalog row; a measured federated kilowatt-hour; a measured cache read; a metered local video box beating 0.45 Wh per GB-hour on the same network; or a serving intensity near the top-down 55.0 Wh per GB rather than the measured 0.45, which would move every video crossover about 120 times.

Epoch AI's panel has a median of 2 prompts per conversation (fp-151) and 4.3 prompts a day (fp-154), with no duration; WildChat's older free-service mean is 2.52 per conversation (fp-095); OpenAI users average 3.67 per day (fp-034). Ten per hour is one every six minutes, slow for someone chatting steadily, so 2.52, 10, 30 and 60 are offered as equal presets; 30 and 60 are heavy-use sensitivities with no field support, and no preset is the answer.

Kind of chat

The text figures stop at 0.60, below GPT-5 minimal reasoning (0.67), Claude 3.7 (0.95) and o3 (1.18) in the same preprint. medium_prompt is Jegham's GPT-4o medium query (fp-021); reasoning is Joule's query 15 times longer than typical (fp-019); high_reasoning is Jegham's GPT-5 high reasoning, medium query (fp-025). Jegham is a preprint that Joule says overstates production figures by 4 to 20 times (fp-020). Grids

Data center grid

Carbon is location-based only. Average, short-run margin, and long-run margin differ nine times for the same kilowatt-hour in the Northwest, so the choice is printed on the figure. Hydro, wind, and solar are life-cycle factors (gf-035, gf-034, gf-033) while eGRID rates count combustion only, and a feed is a declared supply arrangement, not a location factor, unless the machine is physically behind that meter; hydro already committed elsewhere is not clean new supply. Wind, solar, and diesel print no generation water until a claim for them is added. Washington's in-state rate is CO2, not CO2e, and includes exports. The BPA factor is regulator-assigned for power bought from BPA, on a different accounting method from eGRID, so the gap between the two is not a grid effect.
