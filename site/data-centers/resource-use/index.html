The Short Version

The common story says artificial intelligence (AI) drives data center resource use. The evidence supports that story for growth and does not support it for today's total. The figures on this page matter because of where they land, and because the public record behind them is thinner than the debate suggests.

Worldwide, data centers used about 485 TWh of electricity in 2025, up 17 percent in a year from 415 TWh in 2024, when they took around 1.5 percent of the world's electricity; use by AI-focused data centers grew 50 percent in 2025 (International Energy Agency (IEA), 04/16/2026; modeled). Servers fitted with the chips AI mostly runs on were 15 percent of data center electricity in 2024 (IEA, 2025; modeled) and are forecast at 31 percent in 2026 (Gartner, 06/10/2026; estimate). The other 70 to 85 percent is conventional servers, storage, networking, and the cooling around them, serving search, advertising, enterprise software, cloud storage, commerce, video, and social platforms, and no agency or company reports it by service.

Streaming video looks large from the network and, on most published figures, small from the data center. Carbon Trust puts about 1 Wh of a 188 Wh hour in data centers (Carbon Trust, 06/2021; modeled), and the IEA's estimate for 2019 gives them about 5 percent (IEA, 2020; modeled). A 2025 scenario for the UK's energy department put 70 Wh there; its authors replaced that on 07/28/2026 after clarifying what the figure counted, and now put 0.6 (0.3 to 3.1) Wh of a 192.3 (118.7 to 263.1) Wh hour there (Europe Economics for UK DESNZ, 07/28/2026; modeled). A top-down average that spreads all data center energy over all bytes gives far more (Sustainable Web Design; modeled), so the data center's share of a streamed hour is not settled. Video is about three-quarters of mobile network traffic (Ericsson, 06/2026; measured), but traffic is not energy. "AI or video" is the wrong binary; the recommendation systems that run social and streaming platforms are AI, and the missing piece is disclosure.

How to Read the Figures

This page sets out what data centers use, what drives it, how streaming video and AI compare when the comparison is done carefully, what is known about water and materials, and what companies do not disclose. It closes with questions a Tribal government can put to a developer or a utility. Each number carries one of four labels.

  • Measured: recorded by instruments in real operation, such as Google's per-prompt energy or a laboratory's power-meter readings.
  • Modeled: calculated from equipment shipments, power ratings, and stated assumptions. National and global totals are of this kind, because no agency meters data centers.
  • Company-reported: published by the operator in its own report (agency-reported, when a utility or agency publishes it). Some carry limited third-party assurance; none is an independent audit of each figure.
  • Estimate: an analyst forecast or estimate, or arithmetic done on this page from cited figures (marked "derived").

What Data Centers Use

A proposal draws on four things a community can count: electricity, water, land, and the materials in its hardware. The public record is best on electricity and thinnest on land.

Electricity

Data centers worldwide used 485 TWh in 2025; inside the building, servers take the largest share.

The IEA estimates that data centers worldwide used 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5 percent of the world's electricity, and 485 TWh in 2025; its base case reaches about 945 to 950 TWh in 2030, near 3 percent of global demand (IEA, 04/16/2026; modeled). The United States accounted for 45 percent of that use in 2024, China 25 percent, and Europe 15 percent (IEA, Energy and AI; modeled). Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) puts US data centers at 192 TWh in 2024, 4.7 percent of US electricity and 14 percent above 2023 (LBNL, 06/2026; modeled), and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) puts 2024 at 177 to 192 TWh (Electric Power Research Institute, 03/02/2026; modeled). LBNL's figures exclude cryptocurrency mining, as do the IEA's.

Inside the building, servers take about 60 percent of the electricity, storage about 5 percent, and networking up to 5 percent; cooling and power losses run from about 7 percent in efficient hyperscale buildings to over 30 percent in less efficient enterprise ones (IEA, Energy demand from AI; modeled). LBNL puts overhead at 31 percent of US data center electricity in 2024, a national power usage effectiveness of 1.45 (total facility electricity divided by IT electricity) (LBNL, 06/2026; modeled). Density is where AI separates from the rest: the most common rack averages just over 11 kW in 2026, or 7.8 kW without the highest-density sites (Uptime Institute, 2026; survey, self-reported), while NVIDIA specifies about 120 kW for one of its AI racks (NVIDIA; vendor specification).

Water

US data centers consumed 66 billion liters on site in 2023, and the power plants serving them consumed about twelve times that.

US data centers consumed about 66 billion liters (17 billion gallons) of water on site in 2023, up from 21.2 billion liters in 2014, and the power plants serving them consumed nearly 800 billion liters more (LBNL, 2024; modeled). The IEA estimates that a 100 MW US data center directly consumes about as much water per day as 2,600 households, or about 6,500 households once power-plant water is counted (Congressional Research Service, 07/31/2026; modeled). The water section below separates direct from indirect use and turns to the Columbia Basin.

Land

Land varies by orders of magnitude, and this review found no authoritative per-megawatt figure.

Land varies by orders of magnitude, and this review found no authoritative per-megawatt figure. The one campus size here that comes from an official release is Meta's site in Richland Parish, Louisiana: 2,250 acres for a $10 billion AI data center (Louisiana Economic Development, 12/04/2024; agency-reported). A proposal should state acres, megawatts, and phasing together.

Materials

Servers and accelerators carry metals and manufacturing energy and are replaced on a short cycle.

Servers and accelerators carry metals and manufacturing energy and are replaced on a short cycle; LBNL assumes five years for accelerators and tests four (LBNL, 06/2026; modeled). The IEA estimates copper use in data centers at 250 to 550 thousand tonnes in 2030, 1 to 2 percent of world copper demand, and notes that published estimates of copper intensity differ by as much as tenfold (IEA, Critical Minerals Outlook 2025; estimate). A later federal laboratory report puts US data centers alone at 0.6 to 1.1 million metric tons of copper by 2028 (National Laboratory of the Rockies, 09/29/2026; modeled). A later section covers e-waste.

What Drives That Use

Five drivers follow, each with how good the evidence is for its share. The evidence is strongest where an agency or operator measures equipment and weakest where one service has to be separated from another.

Depending on source, year, and definition, AI is between one-seventh and one-third of data center electricity today. Each row of the figure opens that driver's evidence here.

AI Training and Inference

Evidence quality: modeled, by proxy. The IEA uses accelerator-chip servers as a proxy for AI; how large a share that proxy captures depends on the definition.

The IEA counts servers with accelerator chips as a proxy for AI: 24 percent of server electricity and 15 percent of all data center electricity in 2024, and it warns the proxy is imperfect because some AI inference runs on conventional servers and some non-AI work runs on accelerated ones (IEA, Box 2.1; modeled). Gartner puts AI-optimized servers at 21 percent of data center power in 2025 and 31 percent in 2026 (Gartner, 11/17/2025; 06/10/2026; estimate), and EPRI says 15 to 25 percent today (EPRI; modeled). In the United States, AI servers grew from under 2 TWh in 2017 to over 40 TWh in 2023 (LBNL, 2024; modeled).

Training is the smaller and shrinking share of AI's electricity. Google reported that machine learning was 10 to 15 percent of its total energy in 2019 through 2021, split three-fifths inference and two-fifths training (Google Research, 02/15/2022; company-reported). LBNL assumes about 35 percent of AI-server power went to training in 2024, falling to about 20 percent by 2030 (LBNL, 06/2026; modeled).

Video and Streaming

Evidence quality: several modeled estimates per streamed hour, which disagree on the data center part; none for a share of data center electricity.

Video carried 65.93 percent of total internet volume in the first half of 2022 under the measurement method then in use (Sandvine, 2023; measured), and it was the largest downstream category at 41 to 48 percent by region under the method that replaced it (Sandvine, 2024; measured). Ericsson counts it at about 75 percent of mobile data traffic at the end of 2025 (Ericsson, 06/2026; measured). Those are network measurements of bytes. They do not say where the electricity goes.

Where it goes is mostly outside the data center. The IEA puts a typical hour of streaming at around 80 Wh, and its central estimate for an hour of Netflix in 2019 was 0.077 kWh: devices 72 percent, data transmission 23 percent, data centers 5 percent (IEA, 2020; modeled). Carbon Trust found 188 Wh for an hour in Europe in 2020, including home routers, with data centers at about 1 Wh (Carbon Trust, 06/2021; modeled). The BBC's 2024 model for iPlayer gives 65.7 Wh (BBC Research and Development, 09/2025; modeled). A UK scenario revised on 07/28/2026 gives 192.3 (118.7 to 263.1) Wh, with 0.6 (0.3 to 3.1) Wh in data centers; its 2025 edition had 70 Wh there, which its authors replaced after clarifying what the figure counted (Europe Economics for UK DESNZ, 07/28/2026; modeled). Netflix, whose servers sit inside internet providers' buildings in more than 6,000 locations, reports that its own servers used 68.7 GWh in 2025 and that this is about a tenth of its data center electricity including cloud use, which puts the whole at roughly 0.7 TWh (derived) (Netflix, 2025 Environmental, Social & Governance Report; company-reported).

Social Platforms

Evidence quality: operator totals are company-reported; there is no workload split.

Meta used 18,423,634 MWh, about 18.4 TWh, in 2024, 98 percent of it in data centers, an average draw of about 2.1 GW (derived) (Meta, 2025 Environmental Data Index; company-reported). Meta says "AI is driving growth across the company, which is driving increased demand for energy and water" (Meta, 2025 Sustainability Report; company-reported).

The feed itself is an AI workload. Meta's researchers found that recommendation models used up to 79 percent of AI inference cycles in a production Meta data center in 2019 (Gupta et al., 2020; measured) and more than half of its AI training cycles (Acun et al., 2021; measured). No current share has been published, but the measured shares show why a clean split between "AI" and "social media" does not exist.

Cloud and Enterprise

Evidence quality: capacity shares by operator type are analyst estimates; electricity by workload is unpublished.

Hyperscale operators held 48 percent of worldwide data center capacity at the end of 2025, colocation providers 20 percent, and on-premises sites 32 percent (Synergy Research, 04/07/2026; estimate). In the United States, hyperscale and colocation buildings took almost 80 percent of server energy in 2023, and conventional servers still drew nearly 60 TWh, double their 2014 level (LBNL, 2024; modeled). Gartner expects AI-optimized servers to pass conventional ones in electricity use in 2027 (Gartner, 06/10/2026; estimate).

Cryptocurrency

Evidence quality: estimated separately and left out of data center totals.

The IEA put cryptocurrency, mostly Bitcoin mining, at about 125 TWh in 2023, 0.5 percent of global electricity (IEA, Energy and AI; estimate). The US Energy Information Administration estimated US Bitcoin mining at 25 to 91 TWh in 2023 (0.6 to 2.3 percent of US electricity), with a facility-count cross-check near 70 TWh, and later discontinued its survey (EIA, 02/01/2024; estimate). A proposal described as a "data center" can be AI computing, cloud hosting, or mining, and each has a different resource profile; the first question is which one.

Streaming and AI Compared Carefully

Per-unit comparisons are easy to make and easy to misread. Three things need settling first: what sits inside the boundary (device, network, data center), whether a figure is a median or an average, and how many times the unit happens.

Inside the data center, the bottom-up figures for a video hour are near 1 Wh, a top-down average gives far more, and a median text prompt is 0.24 Wh.

Inside the same boundary, the data center, the published figures for an hour of streaming are about 1 Wh (Carbon Trust), 1.3 Wh (DIMPACT), and 0.6 (0.3 to 3.1) Wh (the UK follow-up study, which replaced an earlier 70 Wh), against 0.24 Wh for a median text prompt (Google, 08/21/2025; measured) and 3.91 (2.15 to 7.05) Wh for a query 15 times longer (Oviedo et al., Joule 2026; modeled). The allocation is unsettled: serving energy per gigabyte runs from 0.171 on the UK follow-up through 0.45 on measured hours to 55 Wh on a top-down average, an upper bound and not a literal share (derived). This page therefore prints no single ratio of video to chat.

Generating one 5-second clip with the open research model in the table takes about 944 Wh of graphics-chip energy (MIT Technology Review, 05/20/2025; measured), and an 8-second clip from a commercial model is estimated at 30.8 (19.8 to 43.4) Wh (Jegham, Gamazaychikov, Luccioni, 07/05/2026; modeled), against 188 Wh for Carbon Trust's whole streamed hour, so a clip runs from minutes to hours of streaming depending on the model. The IEA's own illustration runs the same direction: 100 TWh of electricity could generate about 950 million hours of AI video, while Netflix viewers streamed about 94 billion hours in the second half of 2024 (IEA, Energy and AI; modeled). Per unit, new AI media is far heavier than plain text; per prompt, plain text is lighter than it was thought to be. The heavy AI plate on the AI page sets these against each yardstick.

Volume changes the picture: neither chat nor video is the bulk of data center electricity, and the bulk has no single name.

Netflix's roughly 0.7 TWh a year sits against about 94 billion viewing hours in the second half of 2024, or roughly 188 billion a year (derived). YouTube reported 1 billion viewing hours a day in 2017 (YouTube, 02/27/2017; company-reported), a figure for 2016 and so a floor; that is about 0.47 TWh a year in data centers on measured delivery, 0.18 TWh at the UK follow-up's serving energy, and 57.8 TWh at a top-down average, an upper bound (derived). ChatGPT's 2.5 billion messages a day in July 2025 (OpenAI, Duke University and Harvard University; company-reported), at 0.30 Wh a text prompt, are about 0.27 TWh a year (0.22 to 0.55; derived), a floor that leaves out reasoning, images, video, agents, and training. At measured delivery, each of these is well under 1 percent of the 485 TWh total; YouTube at the top-down average would be about 12 percent, an upper bound and not a literal share (derived). None of them is the bulk of data center electricity, and the bulk has no single name: training runs, reasoning and agent workloads, video generation, ad and recommendation ranking, and a large general-purpose cloud. The scale plate on the AI page shows the arithmetic with its settings.

A quick test of any claim about video's share rules it out only on measured delivery figures.

A quick test works on any claim about video's share. For video to account for one-third of 2025 data center electricity (about 162 TWh), the world would have to watch 41 to 124 trillion hours of video a year at 1.3 to 3.9 Wh an hour (derived). All the hours in a year for 8 billion people come to about 70 trillion. At a top-down average of about 158 Wh for a 2.88 GB hour (Sustainable Web Design), the same share would take about 1 trillion hours a year, or about 21 minutes per person per day (derived), so the test rules the claim out only if the bottom-up figures hold. Traffic share fails as a stand-in for energy share for a second reason: fixed and core networks use about the same energy whatever the traffic (IEA, Energy and AI, citing Mytton and colleagues; modeled). The IEA also estimates that televisions, peripherals, and cable television networks used about 500 TWh in 2023, more than all data centers did (IEA, Energy and AI; modeled).

Uncertainty runs in both directions, and no per-prompt figure describes a service a Tribe is being asked to host.

The IEA reports that energy per AI task has fallen by at least an order of magnitude a year, while video generation, reasoning, and agent tasks can cost hundreds or thousands of times the energy per query of plain text (IEA, 04/16/2026; modeled). The Joule authors find widely cited per-query estimates overstated by 4 to 20 times (Oviedo et al.; modeled), and Epoch AI traced the older 3 Wh figure to assumptions that were about ten times too high (Epoch AI, 02/07/2025; estimate). The per-prompt figures come from open models, one company's fleet, or bottom-up models; none describes a specific service a Tribe is being asked to host.

This comparison does not decide a proposal. It says nothing about a site, a stream, a rate class, or Treaty-reserved rights. Those questions are on the Data Center Task Force page.

Growth Trajectories

AI is the fastest-growing load, and the evidence is consistent on that point. AI-focused data centers grew 50 percent in 2025, and the IEA's base case more than triples their use to about 465 TWh by 2030, close to half of its 945 to 950 TWh total (IEA, 04/16/2026; modeled). Electricity use by accelerated servers grows about 30 percent a year to 2030 against 9 percent for conventional servers, and accelerated servers supply almost half of the net increase (IEA, Energy demand from AI; modeled). In the United States, LBNL's reference case reaches 649 TWh in 2030 (11.8 percent of US electricity, range 521 to 843 TWh); in that projection AI servers take 55 percent of data center energy, and data centers account for about 33 percent of US load growth from 2024 to 2030 (LBNL, 06/2026; modeled).

AI is not the whole of growth: other equipment supplies about half of the increase in the IEA's base case.

In the same IEA base case, conventional servers add about 20 percent of the net increase, other IT equipment about 10 percent, and cooling and other infrastructure about 20 percent. Globally, data centers account for about a tenth of electricity demand growth to 2030, less than industrial motors or air conditioning (IEA, Energy and AI; modeled). The IEA attributes the post-2017 acceleration to cloud computing, online media, social platforms, and AI together (IEA, Energy and AI; modeled). The forecasts also disagree with one another.

Water

Direct water is what a facility consumes on site, mostly by evaporating it to carry heat away. Indirect water is what power plants consume to generate the electricity the facility buys, including evaporation from hydroelectric reservoirs.

Direct and Indirect

Power plant water is larger than site water in every account, and the first conversation about water is often with a city or water district.

LBNL's national estimates put indirect use at about twelve times direct use for US data centers in 2023, using regional grid averages and no contract-level data (LBNL, 2024; modeled). The IEA's latest split, for data centers worldwide, is around two-thirds from energy supply and electricity generation, around one-quarter from direct cooling, and the remainder from chip manufacturing (International Energy Agency, 10/14/2026; modeled). Municipal systems are estimated to supply 97 percent of the water data centers receive, so the first conversation about water is often with a city or water district rather than the company (Congressional Research Service, 07/31/2026; estimate).

Per prompt, the numbers have moved: the old bottle figure modeled an older system, and the new one measures a different boundary.

A widely repeated 500-milliliter bottle for 10 to 50 prompts comes from a model of GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centers, which also put the training run at 5.4 million liters, only 700,000 of them on site (Li and colleagues, Communications of the ACM, 07/01/2025; modeled; the source states no data year, and GPT-3 itself was trained in 2020). A medium response in an average US data center works out to about 16.9 milliliters (IEEE Spectrum, 09/10/2025; modeled). Limit: An estimate for a GPT-3 class model on 2023-era hardware, with a server energy the authors call conservative; the source states no data year, so it is not a current per-query figure and not a time series. Google measured 0.26 milliliters on site for a median Gemini text prompt in 2025 (Google, 08/21/2025; measured); adding national-average power-plant water of 3.142 to 4.52 liters per kWh (Li and colleagues; LBNL, 2024) brings the total to about 1.0 to 1.3 milliliters (derived). The old figure was a model of an older system; the new one is a measurement of a different boundary, and neither describes a particular facility.

The Cooling Trade

Every cooling design draws on water or on electricity, and in a basin where electricity comes from the river that trade is not neutral.

Every cooling design draws on water or on electricity. The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC), citing an engineering study, finds that air-cooled systems use more electricity and less water, and evaporative systems use less electricity and more water, both directly and through the power plants behind them (CRITFC, 03/27/2026; modeled). Google reports that its Dalles campus, cooled with water, needed only 6 to 10 percent extra energy for overhead in the last quarter of 2025, against a 54 percent industry average (Google, 2026 Environmental Report; company-reported). Microsoft says about 90 percent of its 2025 owned fleet uses low- or zero-water cooling, and that its AI-optimized design uses no water for cooling (Microsoft, 06/24/2026; company-reported). In a basin where electricity comes from the river, trading water for power is not a neutral choice.

Workload does not set water use; climate, source, and design do. Amazon, for example, uses direct evaporative cooling "where weather permits," and a heat exchanger at the rack for its densest AI hardware (Amazon, 2025 Sustainability Report; company-reported).

Columbia Basin Context

Tribal governments and their commission in the basin have stated what they want measured.

CRITFC, which serves the Yakama Nation, the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs, and the Nez Perce Tribe, says the Columbia is "a stressed water system," that "treaty fishing rights attach to specific places," and that "water withdrawals miles from a river can still dewater a salmon stream." It also cites an engineering study estimating that data centers use about 0.2 percent of Oregon's water overall, "but that does not tell the whole story" because impacts are local (CRITFC, 03/27/2026). The Yakama Nation's findings to Washington's Governor and Legislature say water "should not be allocated to data centers without analyzing the impacts to watershed conditions, including stream flows for fish, temperature, and pollution" (Yakama Nation, 11/10/2025).

The regional load forecast is wide, and most near-term growth is expected from data centers and chip plants.

The Northwest Power and Conservation Council's Ninth Plan workbook puts data center and chip fab load in 2030 at 2,475.1 (1,512.8 to 4,988.7) aMW across its low, medium, and high cases; it does not state whether that is a level or an increment over an earlier base (Northwest Power and Conservation Council, 03/16/2025; modeled). The region uses about 22,000 aMW today (Northwest Power and Conservation Council, 05/02/2025; agency-reported), and the Council expects most near-term growth from data centers and chip plants (Council, 01/27/2026; modeled). Its draft Ninth Power Plan, released 08/13/2026, names data centers as a major near-term driver and adds over 11,000 MW of new generation by 2032 (Council, 08/13/2026; modeled); in one future of that draft, data centers are 20 percent of 11,937 aMW of growth from 2025 to 2046 (Northwest Power and Conservation Council, 08/17/2026; modeled). Oregon's Public Utility Commission estimates data centers at roughly 15 percent of investor-owned utility load today and 25 percent by 2030, and reports the Council's estimate that regional data center load roughly doubles by 2030, mostly on consumer-owned utilities (Oregon PUC, 09/2026; estimate). The Bonneville Power Administration reports an interconnection queue above 40 GW of large-load requests (BPA, Strategic Plan 2026 to 2030; agency-reported). A queue is a list of requests, not consumption; LBNL estimates that data center interconnection capacity runs at about 50 percent utilization (LBNL, 06/2026; estimate).

Site data now exist for two Columbia-region towns, and the two sets for The Dalles measure different things.

Google reports that its Dalles campus withdrew 654 million gallons in 2025, discharged 185 million, and consumed 469 million (Google, 2026 Environmental Report; company-reported). City records obtained by Oregon Public Broadcasting (OPB) show the city delivered 384 million gallons to Google in 2023 and 434 million in 2024, about a third of the water the city uses (OPB, 01/23/2026; city records). The two sets measure different things, water consumed and water delivered, and the city disputes OPB's account of why it is expanding its reservoir. Microsoft reports that its Quincy, Washington, campus used 1.38 TWh in fiscal 2025 and withdrew 1,292 ML (about 341 million gallons), 74 percent of it non-potable (Microsoft, 2026 Data Fact Sheet; company-reported).

One accounting point matters in a hydropower region: how much of a reservoir's evaporation belongs to a new load is a convention, not a measurement.

LBNL's method counts evaporation from hydroelectric reservoirs as indirect water use and leaves out power purchase agreements and on-site generation (LBNL, 2024). How much of a reservoir's evaporation belongs to a new load is a convention, not a measurement, and a utility should say which convention it uses.

Materials and E-Waste

Estimates of e-waste from AI hardware diverge sharply. A 2024 study in Nature Computational Science models 1.2 to 5.0 million tonnes of cumulative generative-AI e-waste from 2020 to 2030 and finds that circular-economy measures could cut it by 16 to 86 percent (Wang and colleagues, 10/28/2024; modeled). A 2026 recalculation estimates AI servers at 131 to 225 thousand tonnes a year by 2030 and concludes AI "may contribute less to global e-waste than previously anticipated" (de Vries-Gao, Resources, Conservation and Recycling, 04/01/2026; modeled). A white paper from the Basel Action Network, counting all AI infrastructure electronics rather than only servers, estimates 8.6 to 13.1 million tonnes per year by 2030, roughly 40 to 60 times that recalculation (Basel Action Network, 09/16/2026; modeled). For scale, the world produced 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, only 22.3 percent of it documented as collected and recycled, and the United Nations expects 82 million tonnes by 2030 (UN Global E-waste Monitor 2024; measured and modeled).

Embodied impacts sit upstream and are the part a host community sees least.

Google reports that its supply-chain emissions, 80 percent of its footprint, rose 25 percent in 2025, mainly from manufacturing and shipping data center hardware and from building data centers (Google, 2026 Environmental Report; company-reported). Recycling returns little of some materials: the same UN report finds e-waste recycling meets only about 1 percent of rare earth demand. What a host can ask about is turnover: how often the hardware is replaced, who takes it back, and where it goes.

What Is Not Known

Nobody publishes data center electricity by service. The IEA reports that "there is no comprehensive data on the share of different kinds of workloads," that operators "often have limited visibility over the specific workloads running in their facilities," and that estimates of AI's share "vary widely" (IEA, Box 2.1). The Government Accountability Office found the same: companies are "generally not reporting details" of generative AI's energy and water use, and it is unclear what share of data center electricity belongs to it (GAO-25-107172, 04/22/2025). The IEA also calls for "more systematic energy consumption disclosures" from the technology sector (IEA, 04/16/2026).

Method limits compound this: totals are modeled from chip shipments, and what one proposed building will use is knowable only if the developer and the utility say so in writing.

National and global totals are modeled from chip shipments, not metered, and the IEA notes "substantial uncertainty both about data centre consumption today and in the future" (IEA, Energy demand from AI). LBNL excludes cryptocurrency, and its water and emissions method leaves out power purchase agreements and on-site generation. One independent estimate of AI's total water footprint for 2025 spans 312 to 765 billion liters, a range that wide reflects missing data more than disagreement (de Vries-Gao, Patterns, 01/09/2026; modeled, abstract only). What a single proposed building will use is knowable, but only if the developer and the utility say so in writing.

What This Means for Tribal Decisions

Tribal Nations are sovereign governments, and each will decide for itself whether a data center fits its homelands, waters, and relatives. The figures above support three requests in any first meeting: a number in the units that matter (megawatts, gallons, acres), the method behind it, and a commitment to keep reporting it. The first meeting does not need to wait for better public data. Ask the developer for each item in the disclosure table above, for this site, and get the answers in writing.

Questions for a Developer

Load and workload

  • What are the nameplate capacity in megawatts, the expected average load in megawatt-hours a year, and the phases by year?
  • What share of the load will be AI training, AI inference, general cloud, or cryptocurrency mining, and will the racks be conventional (about 8 to 11 kW) or AI-class (about 120 kW)?

Water

  • Which cooling design will be used, and what are annual water withdrawn, consumed, and discharged in gallons, with peak-day demand? Which definition of water usage effectiveness is used (withdrawal or consumption, IT energy or total energy)?
  • What is the source (surface, groundwater, or municipal; potable or not), what volume does the current permit allow, and how is it connected to nearby salmon-bearing streams? CRITFC's question list asks the same (CRITFC).
  • What is the discharge's volume, temperature, and chemistry, where does it go, and can the receiving treatment plant handle it in drought, in low-flow years, and if the project grows?

Power and flexibility

  • Will the facility draw from the grid, generate its own power, or both, with what fuel, and for how many hours a year will backup generators run?
  • Can the facility reduce load on request? A test in Arizona cut a data center's peak-hour use by about a quarter (Council, 01/27/2026; reported by the Council).

Disclosure, hardware, and land

  • Will the developer publish electricity and water by site each year, verified independently, and keep doing so after a sale, a lease, or a change of tenant?
  • What is the hardware refresh cycle, who takes the old equipment back, and where does it go?
  • How many acres, in what phases, and who reviews cultural resources before ground is broken?

Questions for a Utility

  • What load does the utility forecast for the project (average megawatts and load factor), and how does it compare with the Council's data center and chip fab input of 1,512.8 to 4,988.7 average megawatts in 2030?
  • Where does the power come from in a normal year and in a low-water year, and which convention does the utility use for hydropower's indirect water and emissions?
  • Where is the project in the interconnection queue, and how does the utility treat speculative requests?
  • Who pays for new generation, transmission, and substations, and which minimum-payment and exit terms protect other customers?
  • Will the utility consult the Tribal government before it commits, rather than after?

For Counsel

  • Which Treaty-reserved rights, water rights, or cultural resources could a proposed withdrawal, discharge, or generating facility touch, and how should consultation be structured to reach them?
  • Who owns and governs the monitoring data for the site's water and power, and on what terms may it be shared?

A fuller checklist, and a tool that turns a proposal's megawatts and load factor into annual energy, share of the region, and site water, are under Questions for Tribal Leaders.
