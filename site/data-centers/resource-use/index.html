Data Centers

What a data center uses, how well it is known, and what to ask for

Electricity, water, land, and hardware: what a data center draws, how well each figure is known, and what a Tribe can ask a developer or a utility to put in writing. Every figure says whether it was measured or modeled.

10/29/2026 Sources checked Sources for this page

Questions this page answers

How much of an hour of video happens inside the data center?

About 1 Wh of a 188 Wh hour on Carbon Trust's account, and 0.6 of 192.3 Wh on the UK follow-up.

Is AI why data centers are growing?

For growth, yes; for the total, not yet: AI-focused data centers grew 50 percent in 2025, while servers with AI chips drew 15 percent of the electricity in 2024.

How much water, counting the power plant?

About 66 billion liters on site in 2023, and nearly 800 billion more at the power plants: about 12 times as much.

What can a utility or developer be asked to disclose?

Electricity and water for this site, every year, in writing; no company report reviewed here splits either by workload.

  • Developer: load and workload What are the nameplate capacity in megawatts, the expected average load in megawatt-hours a year, and the phases by year?

  • Developer: water Which cooling design will be used, and what are annual water withdrawn, consumed, and discharged in gallons, with peak-day demand? Which definition of water usage effectiveness is used (withdrawal or consumption, IT energy or total energy)?

  • Developer: power and flexibility Will the facility draw from the grid, generate its own power, or both, with what fuel, and for how many hours a year will backup generators run?

  • Developer: disclosure, hardware, and land Will the developer publish electricity and water by site each year, verified independently, and keep doing so after a sale, a lease, or a change of tenant?

  • Utility What load does the utility forecast for the project (average megawatts and load factor), and how does it compare with the Council's data center and chip fab input of 1,512.8 to 4,988.7 average megawatts in 2030?

  • Counsel Which Treaty-reserved rights, water rights, or cultural resources could a proposed withdrawal, discharge, or generating facility touch, and how should consultation be structured to reach them?

What Data Centers Use

A proposal draws on four things a community can count: electricity, water, land, and the materials in its hardware. The public record is best on electricity and thinnest on land.

Electricity

Data centers worldwide used 485 TWh in 2025; inside the building, servers take the largest share.

The IEA estimates that data centers worldwide used 415 TWh in 2024, about 1.5 percent of the world's electricity, and 485 TWh in 2025; its base case reaches about 945 to 950 TWh in 2030, near 3 percent of global demand (modeled). Limit: The IEA text says 950 TWh for 2030 while its own annex table prints 945 TWh; both are the same central case, so the page shows about 945 to 950. The United States accounted for 45 percent of that use in 2024, China 25 percent, and Europe 15 percent (modeled). Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) puts US data centers at 192 TWh in 2024, 4.7 percent of US electricity and 14 percent above 2023 (modeled), and the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) puts 2024 at 177 to 192 TWh (modeled). LBNL's figures exclude cryptocurrency mining, as do the IEA's.

Inside the building, servers take about 60 percent of the electricity, storage about 5 percent, and networking up to 5 percent; cooling systems take from about 7 percent in efficient hyperscale buildings to over 30 percent in less efficient enterprise ones (modeled). LBNL puts overhead at 31 percent of US data center electricity in 2024, a national power usage effectiveness of 1.45 (total facility electricity divided by IT electricity; modeled). Density is where AI separates from the rest: the most common rack averages just over 11 kW in 2026, or 7.8 kW without the highest-density sites (survey, self-reported), while NVIDIA specifies about 120 kW for one of its AI racks (vendor specification).

Water

US data centers consumed 66 billion liters on site in 2023, and the power plants serving them consumed about 12 times that.

US data centers consumed about 66 billion liters (17 billion gallons) of water on site in 2023, up from 21.2 billion liters in 2014, and the power plants serving them consumed nearly 800 billion liters more, about 12 times the on-site figure (modeled). The IEA estimates that a 100 MW US data center directly consumes about as much water per day as 2,600 households, or about 6,500 households once power-plant water is counted (modeled). The water section below separates direct from indirect use and turns to the Columbia Basin.

Land

Land varies by orders of magnitude, and this review found no authoritative per-megawatt figure.

Land varies by orders of magnitude, and this review found no authoritative per-megawatt figure. The one campus size here that comes from an official release is Meta's site in Richland Parish, Louisiana: 2,250 acres for a $10 billion AI data center as announced (agency-reported). Meta's own post of 07/13/2026 puts its expansion of the site at more than $50 billion in the region and gives no acreage (company-reported). A proposal should state acres, megawatts, and phasing together.

Materials

Servers and accelerators carry metals and manufacturing energy and are replaced on a short cycle.

Servers and accelerators carry metals and manufacturing energy and are replaced on a short cycle; LBNL assumes five years for accelerators and tests four (modeled). The IEA estimates copper use in data centers at 250 to 550 thousand tonnes in 2030, 1 to 2 percent of world copper demand, and notes that published estimates of copper intensity differ by as much as tenfold (estimate). A later federal laboratory report puts US data centers alone at 0.6 to 1.1 million metric tons of copper by 2028 (modeled). A later section covers e-waste.

Growth to 2030

AI is the fastest-growing load, but other equipment supplies about half of the increase in the IEA's base case, and the forecasts disagree.

AI is the fastest-growing load, and the evidence is consistent on that point. AI-focused data centers grew 50 percent in 2025, and the IEA's base case more than triples their use to about 465 TWh by 2030, close to half of its 945 to 950 TWh total (modeled). In the IEA's base case, electricity use by accelerated servers worldwide is projected to grow about 30 percent a year to 2030 against 9 percent for conventional servers, and accelerated servers account for almost half of the net increase in data center electricity use (modeled). In the United States, LBNL's reference case reaches 649 TWh in 2030 (11.8 percent of US electricity, range 521 to 843 TWh); in that projection AI servers take 55 percent of data center energy, and data centers account for about 33 percent of US load growth from 2024 to 2030 (modeled).

In the same IEA base case, conventional servers add about 20 percent of the net increase, other IT equipment about 10 percent, and cooling and other infrastructure about 20 percent. Globally, data centers account for about 10 percent of electricity demand growth to 2030, less than industrial motors or air conditioning (modeled). The IEA attributes the post-2017 acceleration to cloud computing, online media, social platforms, and AI together (modeled). The forecasts also disagree with one another.

Materials and E-Waste

Estimates of e-waste from AI hardware diverge sharply, and embodied impacts sit upstream, where a host community sees them least.

Estimates of e-waste from AI hardware diverge sharply. A 2024 study in Nature Computational Science finds that cumulative generative-AI e-waste from 2020 to 2030 could reach 1.2 to 5.0 million tons (the unit as the study reports it), depending on future development settings, and that circular-economy measures could cut it by 16 to 86 percent (modeled). A 2026 recalculation, assuming a five-year server lifespan, gives a bounded range of 131 to 225 thousand tonnes of global AI-server e-waste a year by 2030, with no central estimate and final values that depend on how chip-packaging capacity is allocated, and says AI systems "may contribute less to global e-waste than previously anticipated" (modeled). A white paper from the Basel Action Network, counting all AI infrastructure electronics rather than only servers, estimates 8.6 to 13.1 million tonnes per year by 2030, roughly 40 to 60 times that recalculation (modeled). For scale, the world produced 62 million tonnes of e-waste in 2022, only 22.3 percent of it documented as collected and recycled, and the United Nations expects 82 million tonnes by 2030 (measured and modeled).

Google reports that its supply-chain emissions, 80 percent of its footprint, rose 25 percent in 2025, mainly from manufacturing and shipping data center hardware and from building data centers (company-reported). Recycling returns little of some materials: the same UN report finds e-waste recycling meets only about 1 percent of rare earth demand. What a host can ask about is turnover: how often the hardware is replaced, who takes it back, and where it goes.

How to Read the Figures

Measured, modeled, company-reported, or an estimate, and what each unit means.

Each number on this page carries one of four labels, and its claim id leads to its source, status, and limit.

  • Measured: recorded by instruments in real operation, such as Google's per-prompt energy or a laboratory's power-meter readings.
  • Modeled: calculated from equipment shipments, power ratings, and stated assumptions. National and global totals are of this kind, because no agency meters data centers.
  • Company-reported: published by the operator in its own report (agency-reported, when a utility or agency publishes it). Some carry limited third-party assurance; none is an independent audit of each figure.
  • Estimate: an analyst forecast or estimate, or arithmetic done on this page from cited figures (marked "derived").

What Drives That Use

Five drivers follow, each with how good the evidence is for its share. The evidence is strongest where an agency or operator measures equipment and weakest where one service has to be separated from another.

Depending on source, year, and definition, AI is between one-seventh and one-third of data center electricity today. Each row of the figure opens that driver's evidence here.

AI Training and Inference

Evidence quality: modeled, by proxy. The IEA uses accelerator-chip servers as a proxy for AI; how large a share that proxy captures depends on the definition.

Worldwide, data centers used about 485 TWh of electricity in 2025, up 17 percent in a year from 415 TWh in 2024, when they took around 1.5 percent of the world's electricity; use by AI-focused data centers grew 50 percent in 2025 (modeled). Servers fitted with the chips AI mostly runs on were 15 percent of data center electricity in 2024 (modeled) and are forecast at 31 percent in 2026 (estimate). Taking each share from 100 leaves 85 percent for 2024 on the IEA's count and 69 percent for 2026 on Gartner's forecast (derived; two sources, two years, and two definitions, so not one range). Each remainder is conventional servers, storage, networking, and cooling and other infrastructure, including the cooling and networking that serve the accelerated servers. Both shares are by type of equipment, not by service.

The IEA counts servers with accelerator chips as a proxy for AI: 24 percent of server electricity and 15 percent of all data center electricity in 2024, and it warns the proxy is imperfect because some AI inference runs on conventional servers and some non-AI work runs on accelerated ones (modeled). Gartner puts AI-optimized servers at 21 percent of data center power in 2025 and 31 percent in 2026 (estimate), and EPRI says 15 to 25 percent today (modeled). In the United States, LBNL's 2024 report estimated that AI servers grew from under 2 TWh in 2017 to over 40 TWh in 2023 (modeled); its 2025 update, published 06/2026, revised its estimates of total data center electricity use for prior years slightly lower, mainly on fewer reported GPU shipments for 2023 and 2024 (modeled).

Training is the smaller and shrinking share of AI's electricity. Researchers at Google and UC Berkeley reported that machine learning was 10 to 15 percent of Google's total energy in one week of April in each of 2019, 2020 and 2021, split about three-fifths inference and two-fifths training (company-reported). LBNL assumes about 35 percent of AI-server power went to training in 2024, falling to about 20 percent by 2030 (modeled).

Video and Streaming

Evidence quality: several modeled estimates per streamed hour, which disagree on the data center part; none for a share of data center electricity.

Video carried 65.93 percent of total internet volume in the first half of 2022 under the measurement method then in use (measured), and it was the largest downstream category in every region under the method that replaced it, at 41 to 48 percent by region in Sandvine's summary, although its Europe table gives 49 percent (measured). Ericsson counts it at about 75 percent of mobile data traffic at the end of 2025 (measured). Those are network measurements of bytes. They do not say where the electricity goes.

Where it goes is mostly outside the data center. The IEA puts a typical hour of streaming at around 80 Wh, and its 2020 analysis suggests an hour of Netflix in 2019 typically used around 0.077 kWh: devices 72 percent, data transmission 23 percent, data centers 5 percent (modeled). Carbon Trust found 188 Wh for an hour in Europe in 2020, including home routers, with data centers at about 1 Wh (modeled). The BBC's 2024 model for iPlayer gives 65.7 Wh (modeled). A UK scenario revised on 07/28/2026 gives 192.3 (118.7 to 263.1) Wh, with 0.6 (0.3 to 3.1) Wh in data centers; its 2025 edition had 70 Wh there, which its authors replaced after clarifying system boundaries (modeled). On both accounts the data center is a sliver of the hour: about 1 percent on Carbon Trust's own reading and about 0.3 percent on the UK follow-up (derived), and neither is a meter reading of a named service. Netflix, whose servers sit inside internet providers' buildings in more than 6,000 locations, reports that its own servers used 68.7 GWh in 2025 and that this is about a tenth of its data center electricity including cloud use, which puts the whole at roughly 0.7 TWh (derived; company-reported).

Social Platforms

Evidence quality: operator totals are company-reported; there is no workload split.

Meta used 18,423,634 MWh, about 18.4 TWh, in 2024, 98 percent of it in data centers, an average draw of about 2.1 GW (derived; company-reported). Meta says "AI is driving growth across the company, which is driving increased demand for energy and water" (company-reported).

The feed itself is an AI workload. Meta's researchers found that recommendation models used up to 79 percent of AI inference cycles in a production Meta data center in 2019 (measured) and more than half of its AI training cycles (measured). No current share has been published, but the measured shares show why a clean split between "AI" and "social media" does not exist.

Cloud and Enterprise

Evidence quality: capacity shares by operator type are analyst estimates; electricity by workload is unpublished.

Hyperscale operators held 48 percent of worldwide data center capacity at the end of 2025, colocation providers 20 percent, and on-premises sites 32 percent (estimate). In the United States, hyperscale and colocation buildings took almost 80 percent of server energy in 2023, and conventional servers still drew nearly 60 TWh, double their 2014 level (modeled). Gartner expects AI-optimized servers to pass conventional ones in electricity use in 2027 (estimate).

Cryptocurrency

Evidence quality: estimated separately and left out of data center totals.

The IEA put cryptocurrency, mostly Bitcoin mining, at about 125 TWh in 2023, 0.5 percent of global electricity (estimate). The US Energy Information Administration estimated US Bitcoin mining at 25 to 91 TWh in 2023 (0.6 to 2.3 percent of US electricity), with a bottom-up cross-check from identified cryptocurrency mining facilities (estimated maximum use 10,275 MW) that would use about 70 TWh a year at an assumed 80 percent utilization (estimate). The EIA has since discontinued the emergency collection for its cryptocurrency mining survey. A proposal described as a "data center" can be AI computing, cloud hosting, or mining, and each has a different resource profile; the first question is which one.

Streaming and AI Compared Carefully

Per-unit comparisons are easy to make and easy to misread. Three things need settling first: what sits inside the boundary (device, network, data center), whether a figure is a median or an average, and how many times the unit happens. "AI or video" is the wrong binary; the recommendation systems that run social and streaming platforms are AI, and the missing piece is disclosure.

With the screen and the network counted, an hour of streaming runs from 53.7 to 192.3 Wh by source, all modeled. No source measures a chat hour across that whole chain.

Inside the data center, studies that count the equipment put a video hour near 1 Wh, estimates that divide a total by traffic give far more, and a median text prompt is 0.24 Wh.

Inside the same boundary, the data center, the published figures for an hour of streaming are about 1 Wh (Carbon Trust), 1.3 Wh (DIMPACT), and 0.6 (0.3 to 3.1) Wh (the UK follow-up study, which replaced an earlier 70 Wh), against 0.24 Wh for a median text prompt (measured) and 3.91 (2.15 to 7.05) Wh for a query 15 times longer (modeled). The allocation is unsettled: serving energy per gigabyte runs from 0.171 on the UK follow-up through 0.45 from DIMPACT participants' reported hours to 55 Wh on a top-down average that spreads all data center energy over all bytes, so these are allocation scenarios and not measured shares (derived). This page therefore prints no single ratio of video to chat. Limit on the DIMPACT figure: Carbon Trust's analysis of participants' own assessments under the DIMPACT method; the assessment year is not stated.

Generating one 5-second clip with the open research model in the table takes about 3.4 million joules, or 944 Wh (derived), as measured with the CodeCarbon tool (MIT's video passage does not say which parts it counts; measured), and an 8-second 720p clip from a commercial model, Veo 3, is estimated at 30.8 (19.8 to 43.4) Wh of data center accelerator energy (modeled); the study finds that the energy of one such clip can span over an order of magnitude between models. Limit on the Veo 3 figure: A modeled estimate from scaling laws fitted to open models, not a measurement of Veo 3; the paper is a preprint that has not been peer reviewed. Against 188 Wh for Carbon Trust's whole streamed hour, the two examples come to about 5 hours and about 10 minutes of streaming (derived), but they count different things, so they are two points and not a range for AI video. The IEA's own illustration runs the same direction: 100 TWh of electricity could generate about 950 million hours of AI video, while Netflix viewers streamed about 94 billion hours in the second half of 2024 (modeled). Per unit, new AI media is far heavier than plain text; per prompt, plain text is lighter than it was thought to be. The heavy AI plate on the AI page sets these against each yardstick.

Volume changes the picture: neither chat nor video is the bulk of data center electricity, and the bulk has no single name.

Netflix's roughly 0.7 TWh a year (about ten times its own servers' 68.7 GWh, which Netflix says are about a tenth of its data center electricity; derived) sits against about 94 billion viewing hours in the second half of 2024, or roughly 188 billion a year (derived). YouTube reported 1 billion viewing hours a day in 2017 (company-reported), a milestone it said it reached the year before. At Carbon Trust's modeled 2.88 GB an hour, a 2020 European average, that is about 0.47 TWh a year in data centers at the DIMPACT participants' reported energy per hour, 0.18 TWh at the UK follow-up's serving energy, and 57.8 TWh at a top-down average (three allocation scenarios, derived). ChatGPT's consumer plans carried more than 2.5 billion messages a day in July 2025 (company-reported); at 0.30 Wh a text prompt, those are about 0.27 TWh a year (0.22 to 0.55; derived), an illustration and not a bound: the per-prompt figures describe different products and boundaries, the 0.55 end includes a share of the energy of developing the model, and business, enterprise, and API use are left out. Except at the top-down average, each of these scenarios is well under 1 percent of the IEA's 485 TWh estimate for 2025 (derived); YouTube at the top-down average would be about 12 percent (57.8 TWh over 485, derived), an allocation scenario and not a literal share. None of them is the bulk of data center electricity, and the bulk has no single name: training runs, reasoning and agent workloads, video generation, ad and recommendation ranking, and a large general-purpose cloud. The scale plate on the AI page shows the arithmetic with its settings.

A quick test of any claim about video's share rules it out only on the figures that count the equipment.

A quick test works on any claim about video's share. For video to account for one-third of 2025 data center electricity (about 162 TWh, 485 over 3, derived), the world would have to watch 52 to 124 trillion hours of video a year at 1.3 Wh (DIMPACT) to 3.1 Wh (the UK follow-up's high case) an hour (162 TWh over each, derived). All the hours in a year for 8 billion people, a round figure, come to about 70 trillion (8 billion times 8,760 hours, derived). At a top-down average of 55 Wh per GB, a 2.88 GB hour is about 158 Wh (55 times 2.88, derived), and the same share would take about 1 trillion hours a year (162 TWh over 158 Wh, derived), or about 21 minutes per person per day (1 trillion hours over 8 billion people over 365 days, derived). The hours needed fall as the assumed energy per hour rises, so on our reading the test rules the claim out only if the figures that count the equipment hold. Limit on the top-down figure: The high end is a top-down, all-workload average multiplied by a streaming data rate. It exceeds the whole-chain figures of BBC (65.7 Wh) and Ofcom (113 Wh with the device), so it cannot be a literal per-hour data center share; it is the top of an unsettled allocation range, a scenario and not a bound. Traffic share fails as a stand-in for energy share for a second reason: fixed and core networks use about the same energy whatever the traffic (citing Mytton and colleagues; modeled). The IEA also estimates that televisions, peripherals, and cable television networks used about 500 TWh in 2023, more than all data centers did (modeled).

Uncertainty runs in both directions, and no per-prompt figure describes a service a Tribe is being asked to host.

The IEA reports that energy per AI task has fallen by at least an order of magnitude a year, while video generation, reasoning, and agent tasks can cost hundreds or thousands of times the energy per query of plain text (modeled). The Joule authors find widely cited per-query estimates overstated by 4 to 20 times (modeled), and Epoch AI found a typical GPT-4o query about ten times lower than the older 3 Wh figure, from more efficient models and hardware and an overly pessimistic token count in the original (estimate). The per-prompt figures come from open models, one company's fleet, or bottom-up models; none describes a specific service a Tribe is being asked to host.

This comparison does not decide a proposal. It says nothing about a site, a stream, a rate class, or Treaty-reserved rights. Those questions are on the Data Center Task Force page.

Water

Direct water is what a facility consumes on site, mostly by evaporating it to carry heat away. Indirect water is what power plants consume to generate the electricity the facility buys, including evaporation from hydroelectric reservoirs.

Direct and Indirect

Power plant water is larger than site water in every account, and the first conversation about water is often with a city or water district.

LBNL's national estimates put indirect use at about 12 times direct use for US data centers in 2023, using regional grid averages and no contract-level data (modeled). The IEA's latest split, for data centers worldwide, is around two-thirds from energy supply and electricity generation, around one-quarter from direct cooling, and the remainder from chip manufacturing (modeled). Municipal systems are estimated to supply 97 percent of the water data centers receive, so the first conversation about water is often with a city or water district rather than the company (estimate).

Per prompt, the numbers have moved: the old bottle figure modeled an older system, and the new one measures a different boundary.

A widely repeated 500-milliliter bottle for roughly 10 to 50 medium-length responses, depending on when and where the model is deployed, comes from a model of GPT-3 (modeled; the source states no data year, and GPT-3 itself was trained in 2020). The same paper estimates that training GPT-3 in Microsoft's US data centers can consume 5.4 million liters, only 700,000 of them on site. A medium response in an average US data center works out to about 16.9 milliliters (modeled). Limit: An estimate for a GPT-3 class model on 2023-era hardware, with a server energy the authors call conservative; the source states no data year, so it is not a current per-query figure and not a time series. Google measured 0.26 milliliters on site for a median Gemini text prompt in 2025 (measured); as an illustration only, adding power-plant water at 3.142 liters per kWh, the US average Li and colleagues use, and at 4.52 liters per kWh, the LBNL figure for the grid mix at US data center locations, to Google's 0.24 watt-hours per prompt brings the total to about 1.0 to 1.3 milliliters (derived; not a measurement of Gemini's total water footprint). The old figure was a model of an older system; the new one is a measurement of a different boundary, and neither describes a particular facility.

The Cooling Trade

Every cooling design draws on water or on electricity, and in a basin where electricity comes from the river that trade is not neutral.

Every cooling design draws on water or on electricity. The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC), citing an engineering study, finds that air-cooled systems use more electricity and less water, and evaporative systems use less electricity and more water, both directly and through the power plants behind them (modeled). Google reports that its three data center campuses in The Dalles, cooled with water, needed only 6 to 10 percent extra energy for overhead in the fourth quarter of 2025, against a 54 percent industry average (company-reported). Google's endnote compares that figure with the Uptime Institute's 2025 survey average PUE of 1.54, which is an annual average reported by survey respondents, so the comparison sets one quarter at three campuses against a different population and period. Microsoft says about 90 percent of its 2025 owned fleet uses low- or zero-water cooling, and that its AI-optimized design uses no water for cooling (company-reported). In a basin where electricity comes from the river, trading water for power is not a neutral choice.

Workload does not set water use; climate, source, and design do. Amazon, for example, uses direct evaporative cooling "where weather permits," and a heat exchanger at the rack for its densest AI hardware (company-reported).

Columbia Basin Context

Tribal governments and their commission in the basin have stated what they want measured.

CRITFC, which serves the Yakama Nation, the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs, and the Nez Perce Tribe, says the Columbia is "a stressed water system," that "treaty fishing rights attach to specific places," and that "water withdrawals miles from a river can still dewater a salmon stream." It also says an engineering study noted that data centers "may use 0.2% of the overall water use in Oregon, but that does not tell the whole story" because impacts are local. That study projects 2033 data center water withdrawals in Oregon and Washington, with evaporative cooling, at 0.1 to 0.2 percent of Oregon's annual withdrawals, a comparison of scale and not a measure of local impact. The Yakama Nation's findings to Washington's Governor and Legislature say water "should not be allocated to data centers without analyzing the impacts to watershed conditions, including stream flows for fish, temperature, and pollution".

The regional load forecast is wide, and most near-term growth is expected from data centers and chip plants.

The Northwest Power and Conservation Council's Ninth Plan workbook puts data center and chip fab load in 2030 at 2,475.1 (1,512.8 to 4,988.7) aMW across its low, medium, and high cases; it does not state whether that is a level or an increment over an earlier base (modeled). The region uses about 22,000 aMW today (agency-reported), and the Council expects most near-term growth from data centers and chip plants (modeled). Its draft Ninth Power Plan, released 08/13/2026, proposes adding over 11,000 MW of new generation by 2032, and the Council's announcement expects data centers to be a major near-term driver of growth (modeled); in one future of that draft, data centers are 20 percent of 11,937 aMW of growth from 2025 to 2046 (modeled). Oregon's Public Utility Commission estimates data centers at roughly 15 percent of investor-owned utility load today and 25 percent by 2030, and reports the Council's estimate that regional data center load roughly doubles by 2030, mostly on consumer-owned utilities (estimate). The Bonneville Power Administration reports an interconnection queue above 40 GW of large-load requests (agency-reported). A queue is a list of requests, not consumption; LBNL estimates that data center interconnection capacity runs at about 50 percent utilization (estimate).

Site data now exist for two Columbia-region towns, and the two sets for The Dalles cover different years and come from different parties.

Google reports that its data centers in The Dalles withdrew 654 million gallons in 2025, discharged 185 million, and consumed 469 million (company-reported). City records obtained by Oregon Public Broadcasting (OPB) show Google used 384 million gallons of the city's water in 2023 and 434 million in 2024, about a third of the city's total water consumption, or 32.5 percent for 2024 by the city's own table (derived; city records). The two sets cover different years and come from different parties, and the city's table labels its figures as water consumption but does not say whether they are metered deliveries or consumptive use; the city also disputes OPB's account of why it is expanding its reservoir. Microsoft reports that its Quincy, Washington, campus used 1.38 TWh in fiscal 2025 and withdrew 1,292 ML (about 341 million gallons), 74 percent of it non-potable (company-reported).

One accounting point matters in a hydropower region: how much of a reservoir's evaporation belongs to a new load is a convention, not a measurement.

LBNL's method counts evaporation from hydroelectric reservoirs as indirect water use and leaves out power purchase agreements and on-site generation. How much of a reservoir's evaporation belongs to a new load is a convention, not a measurement, and a utility should say which convention it uses.

What This Means for Tribal Decisions

Tribal Nations are sovereign governments, and each will decide for itself whether a data center fits its homelands, waters, and relatives. The figures above support three requests in any first meeting: a number in the units that matter (megawatts, gallons, acres), the method behind it, and a commitment to keep reporting it. The first meeting does not need to wait for better public data. Ask the developer for each item in the disclosure table at the top of this page, for this site, and get the answers in writing.

What Is Not Known

Nobody publishes data center electricity by service. The IEA reports that "there is no comprehensive data on the share of different kinds of workloads," that operators "often have limited visibility over the specific workloads running in their facilities," and that estimates of AI's share "vary widely". The Government Accountability Office found the same: companies are "generally not reporting details" of generative AI's energy and water use, and it is unclear what share of data center electricity belongs to it. The IEA also calls for "more systematic energy consumption disclosures" from the technology sector.

Method limits compound this: totals are modeled from chip shipments, and what one proposed building will use is knowable only if the developer and the utility say so in writing.

National and global totals are modeled from chip shipments, not metered, and the IEA notes "substantial uncertainty both about data centre consumption today and in the future". LBNL excludes cryptocurrency, and its water and emissions method leaves out power purchase agreements and on-site generation. One independent estimate says the water footprint of AI systems alone could reach 312.5 to 764.6 billion liters in 2025 (modeled, abstract only). What a single proposed building will use is knowable, but only if the developer and the utility say so in writing.
