Purpose

ATNI has not adopted a position for or against data centers; the task force gives each member Tribe research, comparisons, and questions.

Resolution #2026-034, adopted at the 2026 Annual Convention in Snoqualmie, Washington, lists Resolution #25-41, “For the Development of an ATNI Task Force on Data Centers,” among the resolutions through which ATNI “has a demonstrated history of addressing data-related concerns,” and dates it to the 2025 Annual Convention (Resolution #2026-034, PDF). ATNI’s convention page places that Annual Convention at Suquamish Clearwater Casino Resort, hosted by The Suquamish Tribe, on 10/12/2025 through 10/16/2025 (ATNI 2025 Annual Convention). ATNI’s committee structure allows the Executive Board to “establish Ad Hoc committees and/or Task Forces to address temporary issues” (ATNI Committees).

The adopted text of Resolution #25-41 is not yet posted in ATNI’s public resolution archive. This page will link the resolution and summarize its resolved clauses once ATNI publishes it. Until then, the task force’s charge is described here only as Resolution #2026-034 cites it.

Two later resolutions frame how the task force fits ATNI’s wider work. Resolution #2026-039 names an existing ATNI task force on data centers as a possible home for artificial intelligence (AI) law and policy development (Resolution #2026-039, PDF), which ties this work to the Artificial Intelligence Task Force. Resolution #2026-034 endorses the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework as an ATNI-recognized standard, and states that “data sovereignty cannot be achieved through data governance alone,” because Tribally controlled storage connected to cloud services governed by foreign law may be exposed to outside jurisdiction (Resolution #2026-034, PDF).

ATNI has not adopted a position for or against data centers. At the September 2026 convention panel on AI and data centers, Johnny Buck (Yakama Nation), co-chair of ATNI’s Youth Committee, put it this way: “the goal isn’t for ATNI to decide whether every tribal nation should be for or against data centers. Different nations are going to make different sovereign decisions” (Underscore Native News, 09/23/2026). The task force’s job is to give each member Tribe the research, the comparisons, and the questions it needs to make that decision on its own terms.

Why Now

The decisions are being made now, in rooms where Tribal governments were not at the table when the process began.

Data centers are the single largest new electricity load the Northwest has faced in a generation, and most of the decisions about them are being made now, in state agencies, utility dockets, and county hearing rooms where Tribal governments were not at the table when the process began.

The Northwest Power and Conservation Council’s draft Ninth Power Plan, released 08/13/2026, names data centers among the “major drivers of future growth” (Council, draft Ninth Plan); the plate above charts its load inputs. Its demand appendix puts the data center contribution to the regional system peak at 1,600 megawatts (MW) in 2027, 3,086 MW in 2030, and 4,009 MW in 2046, averaged across the plan’s futures (Council, Technical Appendix F, Table F-14). The Yakama Nation’s own findings, filed with Washington’s Data Center Workgroup, state that data centers “could require the addition of new electricity equal to two to four cities the size of Seattle by 2029” (Yakama Nation, Findings and Recommendations, 11/10/2025).

The load is concentrated along the Columbia.

In Oregon, data centers are a growing share of investor-owned utility load.

In Oregon, data centers are about 15 percent of investor-owned utility load after growth of roughly 36 percent a year since 2021, and the Public Utility Commission projects 25 percent by 2030 (Oregon PUC, 2026 POWER Act Report).

In Grant County, Washington, eight operators use more than a third of county load.

In Grant County, Washington, eight data center operators used about 280 average megawatts in 2025, about 37 percent of county load, with about 800 MW more waiting in the utility’s queue (Grant PUD, Data Center FAQ, 08/28/2026).

In The Dalles, Oregon, Google’s share of the city’s water supply has grown, and the figures took a lawsuit to surface.

In The Dalles, Oregon, Google’s share of the city’s water supply rose from 12 percent in 2012 to about a third by 2024, and the figures became public only after a newspaper sued for the records (OPB, 01/15/2026).

State governments are now writing the rules. Oregon’s governor asked the Department of Environmental Quality on10/09/2026 to pause air and water permits for new or expanded data centers until 07/01/2027 (OPB, 10/13/2026). Washington’s Data Center Workgroup delivered a preliminary report on 12/01/2025, and the state says “a final report will not be released until the tribal consultation process is complete” (Washington Department of Revenue). The Council’s draft plan was open for comment through 10/16/2026, and its staff are working toward a second draft in December or January and a final plan at the 04/13/2027 to 04/14/2027 Council meeting, a working timeline that can move (Council, Ninth Power Plan status; Council Power Committee, 10/23/2026).

Environmental Impacts

For a fact-checked look at what draws on data center electricity, water, land, and materials, including how AI compares against streaming video on the evidence, see the companion page, What Uses Data Center Energy and Water.

Power and the River

In the Columbia Basin, electricity is salmon policy.

In the Columbia Basin, electricity is salmon policy. The Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission (CRITFC), which serves the Yakama Nation, the Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation, the Confederated Tribes of Warm Springs, and the Nez Perce Tribe, states the connection plainly: “Fish protections are the first thing cut in an emergency; projects that stress water and energy systems put the fisheries at risk” (CRITFC, Data Centers and Water: Tribal Considerations). The Yakama Nation’s findings describe the mechanism: during power emergencies, “salmon protections, which include spilling the migrating fish over the dams, are suspended and salmon must go through the turbines,” and higher loads push dam operators to ramp output in ways that delay migrating fish and expose them to predators (Yakama Nation, 11/10/2025). The Yakama Nation also notes that the four lower Snake River dams produce about 700 average megawatts, against projected data center growth of 2,400 to 4,000 average megawatts by 2029, which bears directly on the timeline for replacing those dams’ services (same source).

These systems sustain relatives: salmon, lamprey, the cold-water refuges that hold them through summer, and the people whose Treaties name them.

The power row of the matrix

Water In

Every cooling design draws water somewhere, and where the well sits matters as much as the volume.

Every cooling design draws water somewhere. CRITFC’s analysis explains the trade: air-cooled systems use more energy and less water; evaporative systems use less energy and more water, both directly and through the power plants that serve them (CRITFC). The U.S. Department of Energy tells Tribes that data center water use “can be up to millions of gallons a day,” though advanced cooling “can significantly decrease water needs, sometimes by over 90%” (DOE Office of Indian Energy, FAQ, 05/14/2026). The location of the well matters as much as the volume: “Water withdrawals miles from a river can still dewater a salmon stream,” and groundwater and surface water in the basin function as one connected system (CRITFC).

The water row of the matrix

Water Out

Most data centers discharge heated water, and some discharge chemicals that municipal plants are not built to remove.

Most data centers discharge heated water, some discharge chemicals that municipal treatment plants are not built to remove, and the volume can overwhelm a small city’s wastewater system (CRITFC). The Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation told Oregon’s Data Center Advisory Committee that “the extreme heat waste and contaminants that leach into the water” are among their concerns, alongside the “noise of fans running continuously” (CTUIR statement, 05/29/2026).

The water row of the matrix

Air

Data centers keep diesel generators for backup, and some new campuses propose their own gas-fired power.

Data centers keep diesel generators for backup, and some new campuses propose their own gas-fired power. CRITFC warns that “back up generation options are heavy air polluters affecting tribal fishers and others along the river” (CRITFC). The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s resource page notes that state and local agencies issue most data center air permits, and that certain emergency engines may run up to 50 hours a year for non-emergency purposes (EPA, Clean Air Act Resources for Data Centers).

The air row of the matrix

Land and Siting

CRITFC’s position is that siting is a regional planning responsibility.

CRITFC’s position is that “siting is a regional planning responsibility, not a project-by-project exercise” (CRITFC). The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes’ Fort Hall Business Council adopted a resolution in May 2026 opposing data center development on the Fort Hall Reservation, on ceded lands, and in Treaty-protected areas, stating “Water is not merely a commodity. Water is life” (Idaho State Journal, 05/27/2026).

Policy Landscape

Tribal

Tribal governments are restricting, hosting, and evaluating; this section records only positions a Tribe has made public.

This section records only positions a Tribe has made public.

Northwest Positions

The Yakama Nation, the only sovereign on Washington’s Data Center Workgroup, filed 36 recommendations with the state on 11/10/2025 covering peak loads, reliability, transmission and siting, water, consumer costs, and tax incentives. Its report also states that “several tribes, including the Yakama Nation, are evaluating the feasibility, costs, and benefits of data centers on tribal lands,” and that the conditions in the report are “essential for protecting tribal resources” either way (Yakama Nation, 11/10/2025).

The Confederated Tribes of the Umatilla Indian Reservation told Oregon’s Data Center Advisory Committee on 05/29/2026 that its participation “is not formal Tribal consultation,” requested that the Governor’s Office facilitate government-to-government consultation once draft recommendations are complete, and said growth “must not come at the Tribes’ expense, or at the expense of our Tribal members through increased energy costs” (CTUIR statement). CTUIR has also said it seeks cooperative siting agreements on ceded lands, or co-management where water, salmon, big game, or gathering are affected, along with waste-heat reuse and local hiring (Columbia Insight, 05/28/2026).

The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes’ Fort Hall Business Council opposed data center development on the reservation, ceded lands, and Treaty areas in May 2026, and called for “early, meaningful and ongoing government-to-government consultation” on any development affecting Tribal lands, resources, or Treaty rights (Idaho State Journal).

Tribes That Restrict

Cherokee Nation’s Principal Chief announced a ban on hyperscale data centers on Tribally owned and trust lands in August 2026, after a task force collected 1,593 survey responses from citizens, of which 64 percent opposed, 14 percent in favor, and 22 percent unsure. The task force defined hyperscale as over 10,000 square feet and over 100 MW. On other land within the reservation, the Nation “will not support any hyperscale data centers on our reservation without proper consultation” (Cherokee Nation task force report, 06/30/2026; Cherokee Phoenix, 08/07/2026).

The Seminole Nation of Oklahoma’s council is reported to have voted 24-0 on 03/07/2026 for a moratorium on generative AI and hyperscale data center development that also bars “any inquiries, discussions and/or developments” with developers of any size during the moratorium period (Native News Online, 03/19/2026). The Muscogee (Creek) National Council defeated a technology park bill with a data center at Looped Square Ranch by a vote of 4 to 11 on 11/15/2025; council members cited vague terms and non-disclosure agreements “that prevent you from talking to your own people” (Mvskoke Media, 11/19/2025).

Tribes That Host or Build

The Hoopa Valley Public Utilities District, the Tribe’s utility, held a grand opening for a Tribally run data center on 07/17/2026 as the hub for its fiber-to-the-home network, a facility its general manager described as “our hub ... for our Hoopa people” (Lost Coast Outpost, 08/05/2026). Colusa Indian Energy, a Section 17 corporation wholly owned by the Colusa Indian Community, announced a partnership on 03/25/2026 to build an AI campus on Tribal land and expand on-site generation to more than 100 MW within 18 months; the company release states no deal terms, water source, or Tribal Council action (Colusa Indian Energy release). The Confederated Tribes of the Colville Reservation are building microgrids their chairman called a “first step towards energy sovereignty” (Underscore Native News, 01/07/2026).

The pattern across these cases: the Tribes that moved toward hosting already owned the energy or network infrastructure the facility depends on. The Tribes that restricted did so on water, land, consultation, and transparency, not on project economics alone.

State

State policy across the ATNI region is moving fast and unevenly, and almost none of it names Tribal governments.

State policy across the ATNI region is moving fast and unevenly, and almost none of it names Tribal governments.

Oregon has the most developed framework. The POWER Act, HB 3546 (2025), directs the Public Utility Commission to create a separate rate class for data centers of 20 MW or more, assign their costs directly to them, and require contracts of 10 years or longer. Under Order No. 26-239 (07/10/2026), Portland General Electric’s residential rates fell an average of 1.3 percent while data centers in that territory, in the Commission's words, have seen an average rate increase of 29 percent; Pacific Power’s proceeding is expected to conclude in late 2026 (Oregon PUC, 2026 POWER Act Report). Hillsboro adopted a 120-day moratorium on new data center applications on 07/27/2026, Salem followed in August, and Clackamas County filed a 45-day notice (same source). The governor’s 10/09/2026 letter asks the Department of Environmental Quality to hold air and water permits for new or expanded data centers until 07/01/2027 (OPB, 10/13/2026). The PUC’s report contains no reference to Tribes or Tribal consultation.

Washington has no enacted large-load law. Executive Order 25-05 (02/03/2025) created a Data Center Workgroup led by the Department of Revenue; its preliminary report went to the governor on 12/01/2025, and the final report is held pending Tribal consultation (Washington Department of Revenue). Tribal representatives were not in the workgroup’s initial lineup; the Yakama Nation asked to join and became its only Tribal member (Columbia Insight, 05/28/2026). HB 2515, which would have required utility tariffs for facilities of 20 MW or more, passed the House 51 to 41 on 02/14/2026 and did not pass the Senate (Washington Legislature, HB 2515).

Idaho enacted H0911 in 2026, adding provisions for new large loads to its public utilities law; it passed 65 to 3 in the House and 34 to 1 in the Senate, was signed 04/10/2026, and took effect 07/01/2026 (Idaho Legislature, H0911).

Montana has no data center statute. NorthWestern Energy filed a Large New Load Tariff with the Public Service Commission on 03/31/2026 covering loads of 5 MW or more, with minimum contract terms, minimum billing, collateral, and exit charges, and with agreements for loads of 50 MW or more requiring commission approval before service (NorthWestern Energy, 03/31/2026).

Public utility districts write their own terms. Grant PUD states that large power users “are responsible for paying the costs of additional generation, transmission, and distribution infrastructure” built to serve them, and that core customers keep priority access to its lowest-cost hydropower (Grant PUD).

What these frameworks share is a rate-design answer to a cost-shifting problem. What they lack is a consultation step. CTUIR’s request to Oregon, that the Governor’s Office facilitate formal consultation before recommendations are finalized, is a usable model for every state in the region (CTUIR statement).

Federal

Federal policy favors speed, and the review that protects cultural places is itself under revision.

Federal policy favors speed.

  • Executive Order 14318, “Accelerating Federal Permitting of Data Center Infrastructure” (07/23/2025), defines “Qualifying Projects” as data centers with at least $500 million in capital expenditure or more than 100 MW of new load.
  • It directs agencies to report usable National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) categorical exclusions within 10 days, asks the Council on Environmental Quality to develop new ones, and presumes that federal funding under 50 percent of project cost is not “substantial federal control.”
  • It revokes Executive Order 14141, whose site-selection process had included Tribal consultation clauses. The words “Tribal,” “Tribe,” “Indigenous,” and “historic preservation” do not appear in its text (EO 14318, Federal Register; EO 14141).
  • America’s AI Action Plan recommends new NEPA categorical exclusions for data centers, broader FAST-41 coverage, a possible nationwide Clean Water Act Section 404 permit, and making “Federal lands available for data center construction and the construction of power generation infrastructure” (America’s AI Action Plan, pp. 14-15).
  • The Department of Energy named four federal sites for AI data centers in July 2025, including Idaho National Laboratory, where it issued a request for applications on 09/08/2025 (DOE, 07/24/2025; DOE, Idaho National Laboratory).
  • The review that protects cultural places is itself under revision. On 07/24/2026, the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation voted to move forward with a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking on the Section 106 regulations at 36 CFR Part 800; its announcement does not mention Tribes or Tribal Historic Preservation Officers, and the draft goes to Office of Management and Budget review before publication (ACHP, 07/24/2026).
  • The Department of the Interior issued Secretary’s Order 3452 on the “timely completion” of National Historic Preservation Act reviews on 08/20/2026 (DOI, Secretary’s Order 3452).

Federal agencies also offer Tribes a path toward hosting. The Department of Energy’s Office of Indian Energy launched a Tribal Data Center Exploration Cohort in July 2026, with diligence questionnaires for Tribes and for developers, free technical assistance, and legal assistance to evaluate deals (DOE Office of Indian Energy). Its guidance states that Tribes “can and should decide for themselves” whether a project fits, that permanent staffing runs “on the scale of a few to a few dozen,” and that selling power to a data center through a utility “may not require you to develop the data center on your trust land” (DOE FAQ).

Questions for counsel, not conclusions:

  • Executive Order 13175 on Tribal consultation remains the stated baseline (EPA summary). When a data center is sited on private or state land with no federal permit, funding, or land, what consultation duty, if any, attaches, and through which statute?
  • If a federal agency ends a Section 106 consultation on a deadline before a Tribe’s concerns are resolved, what remedies remain under the National Historic Preservation Act, NEPA, Treaty rights, or the trust responsibility?
  • Oregon, Washington, Idaho, and Montana sit outside the regional grid operators that the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission directly regulates. For Northwest Tribes, is the Bonneville Power Administration the venue where large-load interconnection and cost allocation will be decided, and what consultation does BPA owe at the policy stage? This connects to Resolution #2024-35 on Tribal leadership in grid governance and transmission planning (Resolution #2024-35, PDF).

Sovereign and Federated Alternatives

Where a Nation’s data lives and computes is a different question from whether to host a hyperscale campus.

Resolution #2026-034 holds that data sovereignty cannot be achieved through data governance alone. Tribally controlled storage connected to cloud services governed by foreign law may be exposed to outside jurisdiction, and algorithms that transform data beyond the reach of Tribal governance can undermine self-determination. Where data is stored and processed is itself a governance decision.

The question “should we host a hyperscale data center” is not the same question as “where should our data live and compute.” Resolution #2026-034 answers the second one. It endorses the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF) and its four tiers, T0 Open/Public, T1 Community Network, T2 Negotiated Partner, and T3 Sovereign Restricted, and names four sovereignty domains that must hold together: Data, Network, Digital, and Computational (Resolution #2026-034, PDF). Computational sovereignty means that the authority over where and how a Nation’s data is processed belongs to the Nation. The framework’s design principle for sensitive data is that the computation travels to the data, rather than the data traveling to the analyst; its T3 tier is “data under complete Indigenous control; never leaves community systems” (TSDF repository, v0.9.8).

That principle produces a different kind of infrastructure than a hyperscale campus. Three scales of it already exist.

A single machine. ATNI-GeoBase is a local-first geospatial desktop application that ATNI’s Tribal Climate Resilience program is building for renewable-energy siting. It runs on a Tribe’s own computer and keeps data and processing there; its published source enforces local file and network restrictions and screens sites with distinct exclusions, constraints, and unknowns, so that missing data reads as unknown rather than as suitable. The published snapshot is source only, carries the warning “DEVELOPMENT BUILD - SOVEREIGNTY CONTROLS NOT ENFORCED,” and is not approved for operational use (GeoBase repository). What this scale shows: a Tribe can screen a developer’s proposal on its own system and share only its conclusions.

A community data center. The Hoopa Valley Public Utilities District’s facility, which held its grand opening on 07/17/2026, houses the equipment that runs the Tribe’s fiber network and is presented by the Tribe as a step toward controlling its own data and infrastructure (Lost Coast Outpost, 08/05/2026). Te Hiku Media, a Māori-language broadcaster in Aotearoa New Zealand, trained speech-recognition models on more than 300 hours of community-contributed speech using eight graphics processors in its own building, under a Kaitiakitanga license that limits use of the data to the benefit of Māori people (NVIDIA blog, 01/16/2024). Neither is hyperscale. Both are sovereign compute: the Nation owns the building, the hardware, and the rules.

A federation. When Nations want to learn from each other’s data without pooling it, the computation can move instead. Federated learning, named by McMahan and colleagues in a 2016 preprint and published in 2017, trains a shared model from updates each site computes on its own data, so that the records never leave the site (McMahan et al., 2017). Under the TSDF, a model trained on T1 data needs network approval and inherits T1 limits. Wopasi Pazo, a working group hosted at the University of Colorado’s Environmental Data Science Innovation and Inclusion Lab, aims at “making environmental data more accessible to community members through simple, intuitive interfaces and natural language,” with members from Sinte Gleska University, the Sicangu Lakota Treaty Council, Cornell, CU Boulder, and the University of North Dakota; its public page is at an early stage and does not yet describe its computing design or data terms (Wopasi Pazo).

None of these replaces the electricity, water, or revenue questions a hyperscale proposal raises. A Tribe can hold its T3 data on its own hardware, share T1 data across a Tribal network under its own protocols, and negotiate T2 access with partners on paper it controls, whether or not a data center is ever built on its land. See Data Sovereignty on the Artificial Intelligence Task Force page.

Questions for Tribal Leaders

Further down: Anchor questions, Checklists. Or start a Council Briefing with this view and the consent and water checklists

The checklists below ask what the numbers cannot answer: consent, land, water, power, rates, jobs, data, and exit terms. Three questions anchor this work:

  • Who decides where a facility is sited, and what authority does the Tribal government hold in that decision?
  • Whose power and water does it draw on, and who carries the costs?
  • Whose law governs the data it holds?

A checklist for Tribal leaders before any data center proposal, drawn from CRITFC’s water questions, the Yakama Nation’s findings, Cherokee Nation’s task force report, and the Department of Energy’s diligence questionnaires (CRITFC; Yakama Nation; Cherokee Nation; DOE Office of Indian Energy). Items marked “for counsel” are legal questions, not conclusions.

Consent
  • Have citizens been asked, in a survey, town hall, or referendum, and what did they say? Cherokee Nation’s task force collected 1,593 survey responses from citizens before the Principal Chief acted.
  • Who is the true counterparty and end user: developer, hyperscale operator, or investor? Has the Council seen every non-disclosure agreement, and does any NDA keep officials from speaking with their own people?
  • Does the proposal reach ceded lands, Treaty-protected places, or cultural sites, and have Treaty and cultural resource staff reviewed it before any inquiry is answered?
  • Is “no” or “not yet” still an available answer at every stage?

Land
  • What is the land status (trust, allotment, fee), and does the Tribe have approved HEARTH Act leasing regulations? For counsel: does the existing leasing code cover a data center and its generation, or is an amendment, a Tribal Energy Resource Agreement, or a Section 17 entity the better vehicle (BIA, HEARTH Act)?
  • What lease term is requested, and what happens to the land and improvements when it ends?
  • Which law governs the facility, Tribal environmental, zoning, and permitting codes or state and local ones, and who enforces it?

Water
  • What is the cooling design, and how much water does it withdraw and consume across all operating phases? Is closed-loop or air cooling a binding term, not a promise? Try the cooling instrument.
  • What is the source (surface, aquifer, or municipal), and what is its hydrological connection to salmon-bearing streams?
  • Under drought and low-flow conditions, could withdrawals push streams below what fish passage, spawning, or rearing need? Has cumulative withdrawal with existing agricultural, municipal, and industrial users been assessed?
  • Have Tribal water rights and instream flow needs been specifically accounted for? For counsel: where a reserved or Treaty water right is unquantified, what must be filed, and where, to keep a new large user from being permitted ahead of it (DOJ, Federal Reserved Water Rights)?
  • What is discharged, at what temperature, with what chemicals, into what receiving water, and are there Tribal fishing sites or cold-water refuges downstream?

Power
  • How many megawatts at full load, and who generates it? Is on-site generation fossil-fueled, and what are the air permit and emissions commitments, including backup generators?
  • Will the load add pressure on the hydropower system during peak or emergency conditions, when fish protections are suspended?
  • Who pays for transmission, substation, and fiber upgrades, and who owns them afterward?

Rates
  • Is there a large-load tariff that assigns the facility’s full cost of service, with minimum billing, collateral, and exit charges? Oregon’s POWER Act, Grant PUD’s terms, and NorthWestern’s filing are reference points (Oregon PUC; Grant PUD; NorthWestern Energy).
  • Will Tribal members’ electricity bills rise, and has a Tribal utility or the serving utility modeled it before signing?

Jobs
  • What are the realistic permanent jobs and wages? The Department of Energy puts permanent staffing at “a few to a few dozen” per facility (DOE FAQ). Try the jobs instrument.
  • Are local hiring, apprenticeship, and training commitments written into the agreement, with a public dashboard and audit rights?

Data
  • If Tribal data will be hosted, under what terms? Which TSDF tier does each dataset carry, and does the contract bar the operator from using, training on, or retaining it (Resolution #2026-034, PDF)?
  • Whose law governs the data, and in whose courts?

Exit Terms
  • What happens if demand for AI computing contracts: minimum payments regardless of occupancy, parent guarantees, decommissioning bonds, and reversion of improvements?
  • For counsel: what is the narrowest waiver of sovereign immunity a Tribe or Tribal entity can grant (forum, remedies, assets reachable, term), and could an arbitration or governing-law clause operate as a broader waiver than intended?
  • Has independent legal, financial, and technical counsel, not paid by the developer, reviewed the full deal?
