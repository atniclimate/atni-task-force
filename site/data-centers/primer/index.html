Data Centers

What Data Centers Are, from One Computer to a Hyperscale Campus

This primer walks through what data centers do, from one computer on a desk to a campus that draws power for a city, with one question at every stop: who does this serve, and who controls it? ATNI takes no position for or against data centers.

11/17/2026 Sources checked Sources for this page

After This Primer, You Can

  • Describe each kind of data center by its size and power, from one computer to a hyperscale campus.
  • Explain the cloud as a way of renting computing over a network, run in someone else's data centers.
  • Ask, of any service or proposal, who owns the machines, who runs them, and where the data sits.

Stop 1 of 7: Laptop

Only each stop's heading, gist, and question: about five minutes, for presenting to a council.

One Computer Does the Same Three Jobs

A data center, by federal definition, is a facility used to process, store, and transmit digital information. A laptop does all three. It is not a data center by that definition; it is computing at its smallest, in one person's hands.

Who uses it
One person, or a few sharing one machine.
Power, typical
15 to 30 watts, drawn from a wall outlet or a battery.
Who controls the machines and the data
The owner. Data leaves only when a program sends it.

A council might ask: which of our records sit on one person's computer, and who else holds a copy?

What is a watt?

A watt is a measure of how fast a device uses electricity. A kilowatt is a thousand watts; a megawatt is a thousand kilowatts, or a million watts; a gigawatt is a thousand megawatts, or a billion watts. The jump matters: a laptop draws watts, a server closet draws hundreds, a server room draws kilowatts, and a hyperscale campus can be rated in gigawatts.

A Server in a Closet Runs a Whole Office

A closet or small room, under 100 square feet, with a server, a network switch, and a battery backup on a short rack. A server is a computer built to stay on and answer other machines; a rack is a frame that holds them upright.

Who uses it
Everyone in the office. The server handles files, email, or a shared database; the people at their desks may not know it is there.
Power, typical
600 watts for one server (a modeled average, not a rated maximum). Even idle, it draws roughly 75 percent of peak power while using 5 to 15 percent of capacity.
Who controls the machines and the data
The owner buys it, runs it, cools it, backs it up, and updates it. No outside party touches the data unless the owner allows it.

A council might ask: who keeps our server running, updated, and backed up, and who has the keys?

A Server Room Runs an Organization's Own Systems

An enterprise data center: built, owned, and operated for its own IT. The room holds 100 to 999 square feet of racks, cooling, and power equipment. As of the end of 2025, 32 percent of the world's data center capacity sat in rooms like these.

Who uses it
One organization. Staff, programs, and community members the organization serves.
Power, typical
7.8 kilowatts per rack, a survey average across data centers of many sizes. No source read for this page gives a typical draw for one whole room.
Who controls the machines and the data
One owner holds the building, the machines, the data, and the staff.

A council might ask: what does our own server room cost each year in power, cooling, and staff?

A Tribal example: the Hoopa Valley Tribe.

The Hoopa Valley Tribe celebrated the opening of its NTIA Data Center on 07/17/2026. According to Acorn Connected's published account, the facility houses the equipment that runs the Tribe's fiber network, described as "an important step toward Tribal Data Sovereignty." The page states no size or power rating. (Alternatives other Tribes have considered.)

Inside Every One: Computing, Storage, Network, Power, Cooling, Backup

Every data center holds the same six systems: computing, storage, networking, power distribution, cooling, and backup. Nearly all the electricity becomes heat; cooling removes it. On a laptop, the six are a processor, a drive, Wi-Fi, a charger, a fan, and a battery.

Who uses it
Everyone who uses that data center, whether they own it or rent space in it.
Power, typical
US average PUE in 2024: 1.45. Cooling takes about 7 percent of power in efficient hyperscale, over 30 percent in less-efficient buildings. Backup: uninterruptible power supplies and backup diesel generators.
Who controls the machines and the data
Whoever runs the building decides how it is powered, cooled, and backed up.

A council might ask: for any data center we use or host, what are its power, cooling, and backup arrangements, in writing?

In a Colocation Building, You Rent the Space

A building where customers rent space, power, and cooling for their own machines. The customer owns and manages the servers; the building owner runs the facility. As of the end of 2025, 20 percent of the world's data center capacity sat in colocation.

Who uses it
Any organization that needs more computing than its own building can hold but wants to own the machines.
Power, typical
No source read for this page gives a typical draw for one colocation building.
Who controls the machines and the data
Split. The customer owns the machines and data; the landlord runs the building, power, cooling, and connection.

A council might ask: who reaches our machines in a rented space, and what becomes of our data when the contract ends?

The shift to larger buildings, and a Tribal-owned facility.

In 2014, over 60 percent of US server energy sat in organizations' own data centers. By 2023, that figure fell to nearly 10 percent; hyperscale and colocation together held almost 80 percent. Tribally owned colocation facilities exist. One self-reports a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.28: the lower the PUE, the more of the building's power goes to computing rather than to cooling and overhead.

A Hyperscale Campus Is One Company's Computing at Massive Scale

Purpose-built by one company for its own operations or cloud customers. At the end of 2025, 1,360 operated worldwide, 580 of them in the United States, holding 48 percent of data center capacity. 58 percent of that capacity was held by Amazon, Microsoft, and Google.

Who uses it
The operating company and its cloud customers.
Power, typical
Each is rated at 100 to 1,000 megawatts, enough for 80,000 to 800,000 homes. A rating is the built capacity, not the power drawn every hour.
Who controls the machines and the data
The operating company owns and runs the machines. Users and cloud customers reach them over the network.

A council might ask: what will a proposed campus draw from our power and water, and whom will it serve?

Fuller question sets on the Data Centers page: Power and Water.

Why campuses keep growing.

A standard server draws 600 watts. An AI server built for training models is rated at 10.2 kilowatts. A single rack can hold about 120 kilowatts of AI hardware. Meta has announced a campus in Richland Parish, Louisiana, on 2,250 acres, with a planned compute capacity of 5 gigawatts; that is a plan in Meta's words, not an operating load.

A campus proposal on Tribal land, and what the release does not say.

On 03/25/2026, Colusa Indian Energy and Strata Expanse announced an AI and energy infrastructure campus, with plans to expand on-site generation to more than 100 megawatts. The release states no deal terms, water source, or Tribal Council action. (Questions Tribes have raised.)

The Cloud Is Computing You Rent, Run in Data Centers

On-demand access, over a network, to a shared pool of configurable computing resources. You rent software, a platform, or bare machines. The cloud has no power draw of its own; its electricity comes from the data centers it runs in, under particular laws.

Who uses it
Anyone who rents computing over the network. The cloud vendor is responsible for all operations, including infrastructure and cybersecurity.
Power, typical
None of its own. The electricity is drawn by the data centers where the cloud provider's servers sit.
Who controls the machines and the data
The provider runs the machines, buildings, and security. The customer holds an account and an agreement. Where data sits and who may reach it: questions for counsel.

A council might ask: where will our data sit, under whose law, and how do we retrieve it if we leave?

Fuller question sets on the Data Centers page: Data and Exit Terms.

Regions, zones, community clouds, and edge.

Cloud providers organize their data centers into availability zones and regions: a region is an independent geographic area of three or more zones, each in a separate building. A community cloud serves a specific community with shared concerns. Edge data centers sit closer to the people they serve. In the third quarter of 2026, Amazon, Microsoft, and Google held an estimated 26, 19, and 16 percent of cloud infrastructure revenue.

Check Your Understanding

A laptop, a closet server, and a hyperscale campus: what do they share, and which are data centers?

All three process, store, and transmit digital information. The federal definition covers a facility whose primary purpose is housing that equipment. The closet and the campus qualify; a laptop does the same jobs without being a facility. Back to stop 1.

When a Tribe moves its email to the cloud, what changes hands?

The cloud vendor takes over all operations, including infrastructure and cybersecurity. The Tribe keeps an account and an agreement. Where data sits and who may reach it depend on that agreement and the law; these are questions for counsel. Back to stop 7.

A vendor offers to host a Tribe's records in the cloud. Name three questions that apply and who answers.

Who owns the machines (the vendor), where will the data sit and under whose law (a question for counsel), and how does the Tribe retrieve its data if it leaves. Each comes from the control row at every stop. Back to stop 7.

A proposal says a new facility will draw hundreds of megawatts. What kind of data center is that?

Hundreds of megawatts is hyperscale range (100 to 1,000 megawatts, or 80,000 to 800,000 homes). The question for a council: what will it take from the local power grid and water supply? Back to stop 6.
