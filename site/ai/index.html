Artificial Intelligence Task Force

Tribal Nations govern their own data, their own languages, and their own decisions. Artificial intelligence (AI) does not change that; it changes how easily those things can be taken, copied, or quietly handed to a system no Tribe controls. Member Tribes of the Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians (ATNI) have named responding to the rise of AI one of the chief self-determination challenges of this time (Resolution #2026-039).

This task force, operating under the ATNI Energy Committee, identifies the common interests of member Tribes in AI technologies, laws, and policies. Its work rests on one question: who does a given AI tool serve, and does the Tribe control it?

Key Facts

  • Established by: Resolution #2026-039 (PDF), adopted at the 2026 Annual Convention, 09/13/2026 to 09/17/2026.
  • Operates under: The ATNI Energy Committee.
  • Related: Resolutions #2026-051 (PDF) on youth and AI in schools, and #2026-034 (PDF) on the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework.

Purpose

Resolution #2026-039, adopted at the 2026 Annual Convention in Snoqualmie, Washington, establishes a task force operating under the ATNI Energy Committee to identify the common interests of member Tribes as they relate to AI technologies, laws, and policies. The task force is charged to propose a plan for outreach and education, and to support Tribal consultation with federal, state, and local governments and with AI companies.

The resolution grounds this work in the responsibilities of sovereign governments. Tribes are already using AI and developing laws and policies about it. As governments, they carry responsibility for realizing its benefits for their people, guarding against misuse, and managing its effects on workforce and economic growth, data protection and cybersecurity, health, education, and environmental protection. Tribal interests in AI run through language, culture, spirituality, data sovereignty, Tribal and Treaty rights, and health and human services.

Working with AI: Building Tools versus Offloading Thinking

Is AI good or bad for Tribal Nations? The question hides the one that matters. AI is a human artifact. What it does for a Tribe depends on who built it, whose data trained it, who can read and change it, and who can turn it off. Those are governance questions, and Tribes have been answering governance questions for a long time.

Two ways of working with AI are now common in Tribal government, and they lead to different places. In the first, a Tribe uses AI to build something it then owns: a tool its staff understand, that runs on data the Tribe has classified, and that a Tribal reviewer checks before anything leaves the building. In the second, staff and students type questions into a general chat tool, accept what comes back, and move on. The first strengthens self-determined infrastructure. The second offloads judgment to a system whose training data, errors, and terms of service the Tribe does not govern. Both are happening. The task force’s job is to help Tribes choose deliberately.

Building Tools a Tribe Governs

The strongest examples of AI serving Tribal work keep Tribal experts in charge of the result. The Sauk-Suiattle Indian Tribe is working with University of Washington researchers on a drone and computer-vision survey of Chinook salmon redds in the Skagit River system; Tribal biologists label the training images and verify every image the model flags, and the project was still in development when the university described it on 11/22/2023 (University of Washington). In British Columbia, a 2025 paper co-developed with First Nations fisheries partners pairs video and sonar monitoring with expert review; the model flags uncertain frames for a biologist, each partner community holds a data-sharing agreement that safeguards ownership of raw data, and any community may withdraw and have its data removed (IJCAI-25). On preliminary video from two sites, species identification improved from 72.5% to 81.2% over a standard baseline; the authors say expert validation remains necessary.

Governments are writing the rules that make this kind of building possible. Cherokee Nation signed its first AI policy on 08/21/2025. It permits staff to summarize public information, brainstorm, draft communications, and develop code; it prohibits AI use without quality review; it requires fluent speakers on any Cherokee-language project; and it creates a governance committee that can approve, prohibit, or limit any proposed tool (Cherokee Nation, Anadisgoi). The same pattern appears in emergency management: researchers co-designed a flood damage-reporting chatbot through interviews with frontline emergency managers and workshops held on Tribal land, so that the tool answers to the people who will use it (Tsai et al., 2024).

Infrastructure choices matter as much as policy. Federated learning, described in the 2017 paper that introduced the term, trains a shared model while “the training data [stays] distributed on the mobile devices”; only model updates travel (McMahan et al., 2017). Te Hiku Media, a Māori organization in Aotearoa New Zealand, built speech recognition for te reo Māori and governs the language data under its own Kaitiakitanga License, giving first consideration to Indigenous groups working on language revitalization (Te Hiku Media). These are proofs that a Nation can hold both the data and the computation. The Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF), which ATNI endorsed in Resolution #2026-034, gives this a working vocabulary: AI training is permitted on T0 Open/Public data, requires network approval on T1 Community Network data, is set per agreement on T2 Negotiated Partner data, and is prohibited on T3 Sovereign Restricted data (TSDF repository).

Offloading Thinking

The evidence on the second way of working is early, but it points one direction. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school mathematics students, access to an unguarded GPT-4 tutor raised practice scores by 48%; when access was removed, those students scored 17% lower on the exam than students who never had the tool. A version built with teacher-designed safeguards, which gave hints rather than answers, largely removed that harm (Bastani et al., PNAS 2025). Design decided the outcome.

Among 319 knowledge workers who described 936 real tasks, higher confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking, and higher confidence in one’s own skills went with more (Lee et al., CHI 2025). The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) names the underlying risks plainly: “confabulation,” the production of confidently stated but false content, and “automation bias,” excessive deference to a system’s output (NIST AI 600-1, 07/26/2024). Those risks are not theoretical. When 22 public service media organizations in 18 countries tested four leading assistants on news questions, 45% of answers had at least one significant issue, and sourcing failures were the largest category at 31% (EBU and BBC, 10/22/2025). For Indigenous languages the gap is wider still: a 2025 Inter-American Development Bank study of five models across seven Indigenous American languages found significant limitations in comprehension, expression, and cultural adaptation (IDB).

The ATNI Youth Committee saw this before the studies caught up. Resolution #2026-051 warns that overreliance on AI in education may weaken critical thinking, writing, cultural transmission, language revitalization, and relationships with Elders, and that AI-generated content can make it harder for Tribal youth to verify what they read. Among United States teens surveyed in fall 2025, 54% had used chatbots for schoolwork help and about one in ten did all or most of their schoolwork with them (Pew Research Center, 02/24/2026). Montana’s K-12 AI guidelines, revised 08/2026, put the standard well: improvements in immediate task performance “do not necessarily demonstrate independent mastery, long-term retention, or transfer to new contexts,” and districts should honor Tribes’ existing guidance when AI touches Tribal content (Montana OPI, v2.0).

Principles for Working with AI

The principles below are drawn from ATNI resolutions and from the public evidence above. They are offered to member Tribes as a starting point, not a standard; each Tribe sets its own.

Govern before you adopt. Name the approved uses, who reviews output, and who can say no, before a tool is in daily use. Cherokee Nation’s policy shows the shape: approved uses, mandatory quality review, fluent-speaker review for language work, and a committee with power to prohibit (Anadisgoi, 08/21/2025). Resolution #2026-039 records that Tribes are already developing such laws and policies.

Build what you can inspect, change, and shut off. A tool the Tribe’s own staff can read is a tool the Tribe governs; a general chat service is not. The Sauk-Suiattle and First Nations fisheries projects both keep Tribal biologists as the final check on every result (University of Washington; IJCAI-25).

Classify first, and default to protected. Under the TSDF, data without a documented classification stays at T3 Sovereign Restricted, and only authorized human decision-makers may lower a tier; automated systems may protect Indigenous Knowledge but never expose it on their own (Resolution #2026-034). Decide the tier before anything is pasted into a prompt.

Keep the person in the loop, and keep the person skilled. Students who lost an unguarded tutor scored 17% lower than students who never had one; safeguards that made students do the thinking removed the harm (Bastani et al., 2025). NIST calls the failure “automation bias” (NIST AI 600-1). A review step that is never exercised is not a review step.

Verify every output against a source the Tribe trusts. Assistants confabulate; in a multinational audit, 45% of news answers had a significant issue and 31% had sourcing failures (EBU and BBC, 2025). Resolution #2026-051 names the same concern for Tribal youth. Treat an AI answer as a lead, not a finding, until a person has checked it.

Language and culture require fluent speakers and consent, never scraping. Models perform poorly in Indigenous languages (IDB, 2025), and the National Congress of American Indians has asked the federal government to prohibit scraping or other non-consensual extraction of Tribal data for AI development (NCAI, 03/15/2025). The First Peoples’ Cultural Council and the Endangered Languages Project publish ten questions a community can put to any technology company before sharing language data (FPCC, 06/08/2026).

Prefer computation that stays home. Resolution #2026-034 warns that Tribally controlled storage connected to cloud services governed by foreign law may be exposed to external jurisdiction. Federated learning (McMahan et al., 2017) and community-run systems like Te Hiku Media’s (Te Hiku Media) show that models can be trained without the data leaving the Nation. Where a vendor’s system must be used, the contract should say where data lives, whether it trains the vendor’s models, and how the Tribe exits.

Judge tools by evidence, not by vendor claims. Montana’s guidelines ask districts to weigh credible research and observed results rather than vendor claims alone (Montana OPI, v2.0). The fisheries results above come from two sites and preliminary data, and the emergency-management chatbot from a small co-design study; both are promising and neither is proven at scale. Ask for sample sizes, error rates, and who reviewed the results.

Supplement relationships; do not replace them. Resolution #2026-051 supports policies under which AI supplements rather than replaces critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships. The same standard applies in a Tribal office: a tool that drafts a grant narrative does not replace the program director who knows what the community asked for.

Questions the Task Force Could Take Up

  1. A model policy: Should the task force prepare a model Tribal AI use policy, covering approved uses, review, data classification under the TSDF, vendor data-handling and exit terms, and model-training limits, that any member Tribe may adapt, adopt, or set aside?
  2. Shared building capacity: Could member Tribes pool a small group of Tribal developers, biologists, and emergency managers to build and maintain tools together, with the code and the data governed by the participating Tribes rather than by a vendor or a university?
  3. Contract terms and counsel: Which terms should Tribes seek from AI vendors on data residency, training on Tribal data, audit rights, and exit, and how enforceable are CARE, OCAP®, and TSDF-based conditions under Tribal and federal law? That last part is a question for counsel.
  4. Learning safeguards: How should the Youth Committee’s resource packet (Resolution #2026-051) set evidence rules for the tools schools adopt, and should ATNI ask Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Alaska education agencies to add language, as Montana has, directing schools to honor Tribal guidance on Tribal content?
  5. A first InterTribal tool: Which shared need (damage reporting after a flood or fire, grant discovery, or tracking policy that affects Treaty rights) should be the first tool member Tribes build and govern together, and at which TSDF tier does its data sit?

Youth and Education

Resolution #2026-051 carries concerns raised by the ATNI Youth Committee: that overreliance on AI in education may weaken critical thinking, creativity, writing, and learning, and may disrupt traditional teaching, cultural transmission, language revitalization, and relationships with Indigenous Knowledge Keepers, educators, parents, and Elders. Chatbots built on language models trained on unreliable or culturally inappropriate content can also make it harder for Tribal youth to verify what they read.

The resolution urges Tribal and public schools to exercise caution and set strong ethical safeguards. It supports policies that keep AI a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships. It supports the Youth Committee in compiling a resource packet on critical thinking for K-12 Tribal and public education, and it urges state and federal governments to consult with Tribes on stronger safeguards for American Indian and Alaska Native youth.

What the Evidence Says

Resolution #2026-051 supports school policies that keep AI a supplement to learning, never a replacement for the educators, Elders, and relationships that carry it (Resolution #2026-051 (PDF)). The public research so far points the same way. Its clearest finding is about design: a chatbot that hands out answers can lift scores during practice and still leave students less able to do the work on their own, while a tutor designed to make students do the thinking can avoid that harm and, in one course, outperform a strong class session (Bastani and colleagues, 2025; Kestin and colleagues, 2025).

Three questions run through everything below. Who does the thinking? Who holds the data? Who decides?

Each finding carries a label for the strength of its evidence. Evidence reviewed through 10/29/2026.

  • Strong: a peer-reviewed experiment with a comparison group; for questions of how many people do something, a large national survey.
  • Moderate: peer-reviewed, but limited to one course, one setting, or one kind of task.
  • Early: a preprint, a small sample, or self-reported links among adults. A lead to test, not a finding to build on.
  • Advocacy: a survey or recommendation from an advocacy organization. Weigh the framing.
  • Record: what a resolution, law, policy, or guidance document says in its own words. It states a position, not a measured effect.

Most teens already use chatbots for school. In a Pew Research Center survey of 1,458 U.S. teens and their parents, conducted 09/25/2025 to 10/09/2025, 54% of teens said they have used chatbots to get help with schoolwork, and one in ten said they do all or most of their schoolwork with a chatbot’s help. A majority of teens (59%) think using AI to cheat is a regular occurrence at their school (Pew Research Center, 02/24/2026). Evidence: Strong, for how often teens use chatbots; the survey measures no learning.

Design decides whether students keep the skill. Researchers gave nearly a thousand high school math students access to one of two tutors built on GPT-4: a plain chatbot, or a tutor designed to protect learning. During practice, grades rose 48% with the plain chatbot and 127% with the designed tutor. Once access ended, students who had used the plain chatbot scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The authors write that, without guardrails, students tried to use the chatbot as a “crutch,” and that the designed tutor’s safeguards largely mitigated the harm (Bastani and colleagues, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2025). Evidence: Strong; a peer-reviewed field experiment in one subject.

A tutor built on instructors’ own materials can outperform a strong class session. In one Harvard introductory physics course, 194 students took one lesson with an AI tutor that worked from instructor-written, step-by-step solutions and one lesson through in-class active learning. Students learned significantly more, in less time, with the tutor. The authors caution that structured AI tutoring will not always outperform active learning, and that “pedagogical best practices must be explicitly and carefully built into each such application” (Kestin and colleagues, Scientific Reports, 06/03/2025). Evidence: Moderate; one course at one university, with a tutor the study team built.

Handing off thinking may weaken it; this evidence is earlier. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers who shared 936 examples, higher confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking, and higher confidence in one’s own ability went with more (Lee and colleagues, CHI 2025). Evidence: Early, for young people; adults, self-reported, and a link rather than a cause. In a randomized study of 117 university students, ChatGPT support raised essay scores, but knowledge gain and transfer did not differ from the other groups; the authors warn that such tools “may promote learners’ dependence on technology and potentially trigger metacognitive laziness” (Fan and colleagues, 12/12/2024). Evidence: Moderate; one randomized study with university students. An MIT Media Lab preprint reports the weakest brain connectivity and the lowest sense of ownership among people who wrote essays with a chatbot; 54 people took part, 18 completed the final session, and the posting does not state peer review (Kosmyna and colleagues, revised 12/31/2025). Evidence: Early.

Chatbot answers need checking. In a study by 22 public service media organizations in 18 countries and 14 languages, working with the European Broadcasting Union and the BBC, 45% of AI assistant answers about the news had at least one significant issue; sourcing was the largest single cause, at 31% (European Broadcasting Union and BBC, October 2025). Evidence: Moderate; a large audit of news questions, not classroom tasks. The ATNI Youth Committee raised this concern in Resolution #2026-051: AI-generated content may make it harder for Tribal youth to verify the accuracy, authenticity, and reliability of information.

Companion chatbots reach many teens. Common Sense Media reports that 72% of teens ages 13 to 17 have used AI companions at least once, and that over half use them at least a few times a month. Its risk assessment recommends that no one under 18 use them (Common Sense Media, 07/16/2025). Evidence: Advocacy.

Native education is missing from this evidence. None of the studies reviewed for this page was set in a Tribal school or a Tribal college. That gap is also an opening: Tribal schools and colleges can build this evidence under their own research protocols, on their own terms.

Where Policy Stands

In its final grant priority on AI in education, the U.S. Department of Education declined commenters’ requests for federal safety and parental-consent requirements, writing that “how best to ensure safety and communicate about technology use is optimally decided at the state and local level” (Federal Register, 91 FR 18774, 04/13/2026). Evidence: Record. At the federal level, then, safeguards are left to states and local schools, which makes the Tribal consultation that Resolution #2026-051 urges more important, not less.

Montana’s state guidance shows what that consultation can stand on. It recognizes that Tribes “determine whether and how AI systems interact with their cultural knowledge,” and says that those developing AI policy “must recognize tribal authority and engage in meaningful consultation when state initiatives could affect tribal communities, cultural materials, or data-sovereignty rights.” It also warns: “Improvements in immediate task performance do not necessarily demonstrate independent mastery, long-term retention, or transfer to new contexts” (Montana AI in K-12 Education Guidelines, version 2.0, August 2026). Evidence: Record.

What Tribal Schools, Tribal Colleges, and Families Can Do

Tribal Schools, and Public Schools Serving Native Students

  • Set local rules before tools arrive: A written policy can say where AI is allowed, where it is not, and who reviews a tool before students use it. Montana’s guidance offers a model: “Districts should not deploy an AI tool unless the local review process verifies each of the following criteria.” Its checklist asks what a tool collects, how that information is used, whether it is shared, how long it is kept, and how it can be deleted, and whether the tool uses features meant to keep students in the product, such as streaks or personas that encourage prolonged interaction (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.
  • Choose tools that make students think: Prefer tutors that guide students step by step from teacher-written content over tools that hand out finished answers (Bastani and colleagues; Kestin and colleagues). Evidence: Strong and Moderate.
  • Check what students can do without the tool: Build in regular work done without AI, so that a student’s own skill stays visible to the student, the family, and the teacher (Bastani and colleagues). Evidence: Strong.
  • Make verification a habit: Ask students to check machine answers against named sources, including sources the local Nation identifies (European Broadcasting Union and BBC). Evidence: Moderate.
  • Keep student, language, and cultural data under the right authority: Before adopting a tool, ask whether student work trains the model and who can see it. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics) center Indigenous Peoples’ authority over data about them (Carroll and colleagues, Data Science Journal, 2020). For language tools, the First Peoples’ Cultural Council and the Endangered Languages Project describe the risks of AI tools marketed to communities and offer ten questions to ask technology companies about Indigenous data sovereignty (First Peoples’ Cultural Council, 06/08/2026). The Tiered Sovereign Data Framework, endorsed in Resolution #2026-034 (PDF), offers one more way to set terms, and it obligates no Tribe. Evidence: Record.
  • Keep people at the center: Elders, Indigenous educators, and families carry what a chatbot cannot. Resolution #2026-051 supports policies that ensure AI “supplements rather than replaces” critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships (Resolution #2026-051 (PDF)). Evidence: Record.

Tribal Colleges and Universities

  • Set AI rules course by course, with learning first: Navajo Technical University’s policy, as Tribal College Journal describes it, “leaves AI in the hands of the faculty, and its ethical use in the hands of students”: each instructor may allow AI, restrict it, or ban it in a course (Tribal College Journal, 11/08/2024). Evidence: Record, as reported.
  • Build the evidence Native education is missing: Small studies of AI and learning, run under Tribal research review with data held under the Nation’s governance, can answer questions no current study answers. The American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) lists artificial intelligence among its cyberinfrastructure focus areas and hosts an Artificial Intelligence & Data Sovereignty Working Group (AIHEC). Evidence: Record.
  • Prepare teachers: Montana’s guidance names Tribal colleges among the partners that “may support joint professional learning, research, and educator preparation” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.

Families

  • Ask the school four questions: Which AI tools does my student use? Who reviewed them? What happens to my student’s words and work? How does the school check what my student can do without the tool? Montana’s guidance asks districts to include conversational AI in family communication “about approved tools, local expectations, and how to raise a concern” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.
  • Ask for the thinking: Before and after any tool, ask a young person to explain an answer in their own words. How do you know? Who could we ask?
  • Talk about companion chatbots: Montana’s guidance says AI tools “are not counseling, assessment, or crisis services” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record. Common Sense Media recommends that no one under 18 use AI companions (Common Sense Media). Evidence: Advocacy.
  • Keep family knowledge in the family: Stories, language, and teachings do not need to go into a chatbot. Before anyone types them in, read what the tool’s terms say it keeps.

Questions for the AI Task Force

Resolution #2026-039 charges the task force to “propose a plan to engage in outreach and education efforts, and support Tribal consultation with federal, state, and local governments and AI companies” (Resolution #2026-039 (PDF)). Three questions for that work:

  1. How will the task force support the Youth Committee’s packet while youth lead it? Which help does the Committee want (research, review, printing, or outreach), who reviews drafts, and when does the packet go to member Tribes?
  2. Where should Tribal consultation on school AI begin? With the U.S. Department of Education leaving safety decisions to states and local schools, which state education agencies in the ATNI region should the task force approach first, and what should Tribes ask of them: tool review, data terms, the handling of Tribal content, or all three?
  3. What should any AI tool meet before it reaches the students of a member Tribe? Should the task force draft model terms on student data, language recordings, and cultural material, drawing on the CARE Principles and the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework, for each Nation to adapt or set aside?

A question for counsel, not a conclusion: which federal, state, and Tribal rules govern AI tools in Tribally controlled schools and in schools funded by the Bureau of Indian Education?

Data Sovereignty

In Resolution #2026-034, member Tribes endorsed the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF) as an ATNI-recognized standard for Indigenous Data Sovereignty and authorized its release as version 1.0. The framework sorts data into four tiers: T0 Open/Public, T1 Community Network, T2 Negotiated Partner, and T3 Sovereign Restricted. It defaults to the most protective tier, and only authorized human decision-makers may lower a classification. Automated systems may protect Indigenous Knowledge; they never expose it on their own.

The resolution names AI directly. It warns of Indigenous Knowledge taken into training data without authorization, the reproduction of sacred or restricted Knowledge, deficit-based framings, and the reuse of Indigenous data beyond Indigenous governance. The endorsement obligates no Tribe to adopt the framework; each Tribe makes its own sovereign determination about its use.
