Overview

Purpose: Resolution #2026-039 establishes this task force to find what member Tribes share on AI.

Tribal Nations govern their own data, their own languages, and their own decisions. Artificial intelligence (AI) does not change that; it changes how easily those things can be taken, copied, or quietly handed to a system no Tribe controls. Member Tribes of the Affiliated Tribes of Northwest Indians (ATNI) named responding to the rise of AI one of the chief self-determination challenges of this time (Resolution #2026-039).

Resolution #2026-039, adopted at the 2026 Annual Convention in Snoqualmie, Washington, 09/13/2026 to 09/17/2026, establishes a task force operating under the ATNI Energy Committee to find what member Tribes share on AI technologies, laws, and policies. The task force is charged to propose a plan for outreach and education, and to support Tribal consultation with federal, state, and local governments and with AI companies.

The resolution grounds this work in the responsibilities of sovereign governments. Tribes are already using AI and developing laws and policies about it. As governments, they carry responsibility for realizing its benefits for their people, guarding against misuse, and managing its effects on workforce and economic growth, data protection and cybersecurity, health, education, and environmental protection. Tribal interests in AI run through language, culture, spirituality, data sovereignty, Tribal and Treaty rights, and health and human services.

The comparison is built as a model, because no source measures chat, video, and a short-video feed on one boundary.

Each activity has four segments: data center, network, home router, and device. Each segment is filled with a sourced input or a plain assumption, labeled measured or modeled, and shown as a range. The two segments that depend on what the hour is spent on are shown apart from the two that do not, so a shared constant can never read as a finding.

The ranges run every input’s low and high through the same equations; they are not confidence intervals. The default is one defensible point, not a central estimate. The inputs, the equations, and the computed defaults are published as data (JSON), and the longer reference is What Uses Data Center Energy and Water.

This comparison cannot support a single ratio between chat and video, in either direction.
  • “Chat is so many times video,” or the reverse. Chat, video, and social figures each stop at a different boundary, so the page prints segments and no ratio.
  • “Instagram costs more than ChatGPT.” There is no measured Instagram data center figure.
  • A typical chat day set against Meta’s per-person figure. The two differ in boundary, population, and denominator.
  • Any figure for Claude’s own chat use. Anthropic publishes none, as Zeke Hausfather, The Climate Brink records.
  • Any share of the 2030 Pacific Northwest data center load made of chat messages, or any per-hour or per-prompt figure for a named facility in the region.

The model has known weaknesses, and each one is printed here.
  • Medians times counts: the chat day and the chat total multiply a median prompt by a count, so they are floors, and the only average-class figure has no stated method.
  • The per-user denominator is weekly users over all days; Meta’s is daily active people. The two rows are printed, not compared.
  • The router by time charges a shared, always-on device’s full draw to one person’s hour, the same on every row.
  • The chat device segment is battery rates and a voltage assumption, not a wall meter. A testing firm’s higher battery rate for chat is set aside.
  • The streaming delivery intensity is this page’s arithmetic from a DIMPACT hour and a Carbon Trust data mix. The short-video segment charges delivery only, and no source measures a feed’s ranking compute.
  • The kinds of chat above short text come from modeled figures, including a preprint that a peer-reviewed paper says overstates production figures 4 to 20 times. Multi-turn context growth and model calls per chat prompt are unmeasured.
  • Network intensities are 2020 regressions and a 2024 extrapolation.
  • Every chat data center figure is operator-published or industry-authored, and only Google’s is production data. The streaming defaults are also industry-authored or commissioned, and the DESNZ figure is a government scenario.
  • Prompts per hour is a modeled hour; minutes per day are averages from Android apps.
  • The scale plate uses 2016 YouTube hours and ChatGPT only. The Council’s series is a workbook input, not a forecast of any facility.
A one-method measurement of a chat hour and a video hour on the same phone would change the answer most.

Work

Exposure is not displacement, so exposure counts are listed here and never drawn as loss.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts it plainly: “Exposure does not imply job loss, productivity gains, automation probability, or wage effects” (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics).

  • International Labour Organization: 29 percent of jobs in female-dominated occupations are exposed to generative AI, in countries with data, and 3.3 percent of global employment sits in the highest exposure category.
  • MIT and Oak Ridge National Laboratory: 11.7 percent of wage value in administrative, financial, and professional services is technically exposed. That is technical exposure, not predicted job loss.
  • National Bureau of Economic Research: 26.5 million workers in the top quartile of exposure also work in occupations with above-median capacity to adapt, and 6.1 million workers are highly exposed with low capacity to adapt, concentrated in clerical and administrative roles.
Announced cuts are a count of announcements, not a rate of job loss.

Challenger, Gray and Christmas tallied 120,136 announced cuts citing AI from January through September 2026 and 54,836 announced cuts in all of 2025. The firm assigns the reason, and the tally counts layoff plans announced by U.S.-based employers, not realized job losses.

What a data center itself brings in jobs is a separate question, on the data centers page.

The occupations here cover the whole U.S. economy. The construction and operating jobs a data center brings, and what state tax preferences bought, are on the Jobs tab of the data centers page.

Working with AI: Building Tools versus Offloading Thinking

AI is a human artifact. What it does for a Tribe depends on who built it, whose data trained it, who can read and change it, and who can turn it off. The task force’s job is to help Tribes choose deliberately between two ways of working with it, both now common in Tribal government.

Building a tool the Tribe owns

  • Staff understand it.
  • It runs on data the Tribe has classified.
  • A Tribal reviewer checks the work before anything leaves the building.

Offloading to a general chat tool

  • Staff and students type questions and accept what comes back.
  • The Tribe does not govern its training data, its errors, or its terms of service.
Building tools a Tribe governs: the strongest examples keep Tribal experts in charge of the result.

The Sauk-Suiattle Indian Tribe is working with University of Washington researchers on a drone and computer-vision survey of Chinook salmon redds in the Skagit River system; Tribal biologists label the training images and verify every image the model flags, and the project was still in development when the university described it on 11/22/2023 (University of Washington). In British Columbia, a 2025 paper co-developed with First Nations fisheries partners pairs video and sonar monitoring with expert review; the model flags uncertain frames for a biologist, each partner community holds a data-sharing agreement that safeguards ownership of raw data, and any community may withdraw and have its data removed (IJCAI-25). On preliminary video from two sites, species identification improved from 72.5% to 81.2% over a standard baseline; the authors say expert validation remains necessary.

Governments are writing the rules that make this kind of building possible. Cherokee Nation signed its first AI policy on 08/21/2025. It permits staff to summarize public information, brainstorm, draft communications, and develop code; it prohibits AI use without quality review; it requires fluent speakers on any Cherokee-language project; and it creates a governance committee that can approve, prohibit, or limit any proposed tool (Cherokee Nation, Anadisgoi). The same pattern appears in emergency management: researchers co-designed a flood damage-reporting chatbot through interviews with frontline emergency managers and workshops held on Tribal land, so that the tool answers to the people who will use it (Tsai et al., 2024).

Infrastructure choices matter as much as policy. Federated learning, described in the 2017 paper that introduced the term, trains a shared model while “the training data [stays] distributed on the mobile devices”; only model updates travel (McMahan et al., 2017). Te Hiku Media, a Māori organization in Aotearoa New Zealand, built speech recognition for te reo Māori and governs the language data under its own Kaitiakitanga License, giving first consideration to Indigenous groups working on language revitalization (Te Hiku Media). These are proofs that a Nation can hold both the data and the computation. The Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF), which ATNI endorsed in Resolution #2026-034, gives this a working vocabulary, set out in the tier grid.

Offloading thinking: the evidence is early, but it points one direction.

Researchers gave nearly a thousand high school mathematics students access to one of two tutors built on GPT-4: a plain chatbot, or a tutor designed to protect learning. During practice, grades rose 48% with the plain chatbot and 127% with the designed tutor. Once access ended, students who had used the plain chatbot scored 17% lower than students who never had access. The authors write that, without guardrails, students tried to use the chatbot as a “crutch,” and that the designed tutor’s safeguards largely mitigated the harm (Bastani and colleagues, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2025). Evidence: Strong; a peer-reviewed field experiment in one subject.

Among 319 knowledge workers who described 936 real tasks, higher confidence in generative AI went with less critical thinking, and higher confidence in one’s own skills went with more (Lee et al., CHI 2025). The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) names the underlying risks plainly: “confabulation,” the production of confidently stated but false content, and “automation bias,” excessive deference to a system’s output (NIST AI 600-1, 07/26/2024).

Those risks are not theoretical. In a study by 22 public service media organizations in 18 countries and 14 languages, working with the European Broadcasting Union and the BBC, 45% of AI assistant answers about the news had at least one significant issue; sourcing was the largest single cause, at 31% (European Broadcasting Union and BBC, October 2025). Evidence: Moderate; a large audit of news questions, not classroom tasks. For Indigenous languages the gap is wider still: a 2025 Inter-American Development Bank study of five models across seven Indigenous American languages found significant limitations in comprehension, expression, and cultural adaptation (IDB).

The ATNI Youth Committee saw this before the studies caught up. Resolution #2026-051 warns that overreliance on AI in education may weaken critical thinking, writing, cultural transmission, language revitalization, and relationships with Elders, and that AI-generated content can make it harder for Tribal youth to verify what they read. In a Pew Research Center survey of 1,458 U.S. teens and their parents, conducted 09/25/2025 to 10/09/2025, 54% of teens said they have used chatbots to get help with schoolwork, and one in ten said they do all or most of their schoolwork with a chatbot’s help. A majority of teens (59%) think using AI to cheat is a regular occurrence at their school (Pew Research Center, 02/24/2026). Evidence: Strong, for how often teens use chatbots; the survey measures no learning.

Montana’s state guidance shows what Tribal consultation can stand on. It recognizes that Tribes “determine whether and how AI systems interact with their cultural knowledge,” and says that those developing AI policy “must recognize tribal authority and engage in meaningful consultation when state initiatives could affect tribal communities, cultural materials, or data-sovereignty rights.” It also warns: “Improvements in immediate task performance do not necessarily demonstrate independent mastery, long-term retention, or transfer to new contexts” (Montana AI in K-12 Education Guidelines, version 2.0, August 2026). Evidence: Record.

Five questions the task force could take up, from a model policy to a first InterTribal tool.
  1. A model policy: Should the task force prepare a model Tribal AI use policy, covering approved uses, review, data classification under the TSDF, vendor data-handling and exit terms, and model-training limits, that any member Tribe may adapt, adopt, or set aside?
  2. Shared building capacity: Could member Tribes pool a small group of Tribal developers, biologists, and emergency managers to build and maintain tools together, with the code and the data governed by the participating Tribes rather than by a vendor or a university?
  3. Contract terms and counsel: Which terms should Tribes seek from AI vendors on data residency, training on Tribal data, audit rights, and exit, and how enforceable are CARE, OCAP®, and TSDF-based conditions under Tribal and federal law? That last part is a question for counsel.
  4. Learning safeguards: How should the Youth Committee’s resource packet (Resolution #2026-051) set evidence rules for the tools schools adopt, and should ATNI ask Washington, Oregon, Idaho, and Alaska education agencies to add language, as Montana has, directing schools to honor Tribal guidance on Tribal content?
  5. A first InterTribal tool: Which shared need (damage reporting after a flood or fire, grant discovery, or tracking policy that affects Treaty rights) should be the first tool member Tribes build and govern together, and at which TSDF tier does its data sit?

Principles for Working with AI

Drawn from ATNI resolutions and the public evidence above, and offered to member Tribes as a starting point, not a standard; each Tribe sets its own.

Govern before you adopt.

Name the approved uses, who reviews output, and who can say no, before a tool is in daily use. Cherokee Nation’s policy shows the shape: approved uses, mandatory quality review, fluent-speaker review for language work, and a committee with power to prohibit (Anadisgoi, 08/21/2025). Resolution #2026-039 records that Tribes are already developing such laws and policies.

Build what you can inspect, change, and shut off.

A tool the Tribe’s own staff can read is a tool the Tribe governs; a general chat service is not. The Sauk-Suiattle and First Nations fisheries projects both keep Tribal biologists as the final check on every result (University of Washington; IJCAI-25).

Classify first, and default to protected.

Under the TSDF, data without a documented classification stays at T3 Sovereign Restricted, and only authorized human decision-makers may lower a tier; automated systems may protect Indigenous Knowledge but never expose it on their own (Resolution #2026-034). Decide the tier before anything is pasted into a prompt.

Keep the person in the loop, and keep the person skilled.

Students who lost an unguarded tutor scored 17% lower than students who never had one; safeguards that made students do the thinking removed the harm (Bastani et al., 2025). NIST calls the failure “automation bias” (NIST AI 600-1).

Verify every output against a source the Tribe trusts.

Assistants confabulate; in a multinational audit, 45% of news answers had a significant issue and 31% had sourcing failures (EBU and BBC, 2025). Resolution #2026-051 names the same concern for Tribal youth. Treat an AI answer as a lead, not a finding, until a person has checked it.

Language and culture require fluent speakers and consent, never scraping.

Models perform poorly in Indigenous languages (IDB, 2025), and the National Congress of American Indians has asked the federal government to prohibit scraping or other non-consensual extraction of Tribal data for AI development (NCAI, 03/15/2025). The First Peoples’ Cultural Council and the Endangered Languages Project publish ten questions a community can put to any technology company before sharing language data (FPCC, 06/08/2026).

Prefer computation that stays home.

Resolution #2026-034 warns that Tribally controlled storage connected to cloud services governed by foreign law may be exposed to external jurisdiction. Federated learning (McMahan et al., 2017) and community-run systems like Te Hiku Media’s (Te Hiku Media) show that models can be trained without the data leaving the Nation. Where a vendor’s system must be used, the contract should say where data lives, whether it trains the vendor’s models, and how the Tribe exits.

Judge tools by evidence, not by vendor claims.

Montana’s guidelines ask districts to weigh credible research and observed results, not vendor claims alone (Montana OPI, v2.0). The fisheries results above come from two sites and preliminary data; the emergency-management chatbot from a small co-design study. Both are promising. Neither is proven at scale. Ask for sample sizes, error rates, and who reviewed the results.

Supplement relationships; do not replace them.

Resolution #2026-051 supports policies under which AI supplements rather than replaces critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships. The same standard applies in a Tribal office: a tool that drafts a grant narrative does not replace the program director who knows what the community asked for.

Youth and Education

Resolution #2026-051 carries concerns raised by the ATNI Youth Committee: that overreliance on AI in education may weaken critical thinking, creativity, writing, and learning, and may disrupt traditional teaching, cultural transmission, language revitalization, and relationships with Indigenous Knowledge Keepers, educators, parents, and Elders. Chatbots built on language models trained on unreliable or culturally inappropriate content can also make it harder for Tribal youth to verify what they read.

The resolution urges caution in schools and supports a youth-led resource packet.

The resolution urges Tribal and public schools to exercise caution and set strong ethical safeguards. It supports policies that keep AI a supplement to, rather than a replacement for, critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships. It supports the Youth Committee in compiling a resource packet on critical thinking for K-12 Tribal and public education, and it urges state and federal governments to consult with Tribes on stronger safeguards for American Indian and Alaska Native youth.

The clearest finding so far is about design: answers can lift practice scores and still leave students less able.

Resolution #2026-051 supports school policies that keep AI a supplement to learning, never a replacement for the educators, Elders, and relationships that carry it (Resolution #2026-051 (PDF)). The public research so far points the same way. Its clearest finding is about design: a chatbot that hands out answers can lift scores during practice and still leave students less able to do the work on their own, while a tutor designed to make students do the thinking can avoid that harm and, in one course, outperform a strong class session (Bastani and colleagues, 2025; Kestin and colleagues, 2025).

Most teens already use chatbots for school.

The Pew Research Center’s survey is told under Offloading Thinking on the Working with AI tab. Evidence: Strong, for how often teens use chatbots; the survey measures no learning.

Design decides whether students keep the skill.

The field experiment with two tutors is told under Offloading Thinking on the Working with AI tab. Evidence: Strong; a peer-reviewed field experiment in one subject.

A tutor built on instructors’ own materials can outperform a strong class session.

In one Harvard introductory physics course, 194 students took one lesson with an AI tutor that worked from instructor-written, step-by-step solutions and one lesson through in-class active learning. Students learned significantly more, in less time, with the tutor. The authors caution that structured AI tutoring will not always outperform active learning, and that “pedagogical best practices must be explicitly and carefully built into each such application” (Kestin and colleagues, Scientific Reports, 06/03/2025). Evidence: Moderate; one course at one university, with a tutor the study team built.

Handing off thinking may weaken it; this evidence is earlier.

The survey of knowledge workers is told under Offloading Thinking. Evidence: Early, for young people; adults, self-reported, and a link rather than a cause. In a randomized study of 117 university students, ChatGPT support raised essay scores, but knowledge gain and transfer did not differ from the other groups; the authors warn that such tools “may promote learners’ dependence on technology and potentially trigger metacognitive laziness” (Fan and colleagues, 12/12/2024). Evidence: Moderate; one randomized study with university students. An MIT Media Lab preprint reports the weakest brain connectivity and the lowest sense of ownership among people who wrote essays with a chatbot; 54 people took part, 18 completed the final session, and the posting does not state peer review (Kosmyna and colleagues, revised 12/31/2025). Evidence: Early.

Chatbot answers need checking.

The audit of news answers is told under Offloading Thinking. Evidence: Moderate; a large audit of news questions, not classroom tasks. The ATNI Youth Committee raised this concern in Resolution #2026-051: AI-generated content may make it harder for Tribal youth to verify the accuracy, authenticity, and reliability of information.

Companion chatbots reach many teens.

Common Sense Media reports that 72% of teens ages 13 to 17 have used AI companions at least once, and that over half use them at least a few times a month. Its risk assessment recommends that no one under 18 use them (Common Sense Media, 07/16/2025). Evidence: Advocacy.

Native education is missing from this evidence.

None of the studies reviewed for this page was set in a Tribal school or a Tribal college. Tribal schools and colleges can build this evidence under their own research protocols, on their own terms.

At the federal level, safeguards are left to states and local schools.

In its final grant priority on AI in education, the U.S. Department of Education declined commenters’ requests for federal safety and parental-consent requirements, writing that “how best to ensure safety and communicate about technology use is optimally decided at the state and local level” (Federal Register, 91 FR 18774, 04/13/2026). Evidence: Record. That makes the Tribal consultation that Resolution #2026-051 urges more important, not less. Montana’s state guidance, told under Offloading Thinking, shows what that consultation can stand on.

Tribal schools, and public schools serving Native students, can set local rules before tools arrive.
  • Set local rules before tools arrive: A written policy can say where AI is allowed, where it is not, and who reviews a tool before students use it. Montana’s guidance offers a model: “Districts should not deploy an AI tool unless the local review process verifies each of the following criteria.” Its checklist asks what a tool collects, how that information is used, whether it is shared, how long it is kept, and how it can be deleted, and whether the tool uses features meant to keep students in the product, such as streaks or personas that encourage prolonged interaction (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.
  • Choose tools that make students think: Prefer tutors that guide students step by step from teacher-written content over tools that hand out finished answers (Bastani and colleagues; Kestin and colleagues). Evidence: Strong and Moderate.
  • Check what students can do without the tool: Build in regular work done without AI, so that a student’s own skill stays visible to the student, the family, and the teacher (Bastani and colleagues). Evidence: Strong.
  • Make verification a habit: Students should check machine answers against named sources, including sources the local Nation identifies (European Broadcasting Union and BBC). Evidence: Moderate.
  • Keep student, language, and cultural data under the right authority: Before adopting a tool, ask whether student work trains the model and who can see it. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics) center Indigenous Peoples’ authority over data about them (Carroll and colleagues, Data Science Journal, 2020). For language tools, the First Peoples’ Cultural Council and the Endangered Languages Project describe the risks of AI tools marketed to communities and offer ten questions to ask technology companies about Indigenous data sovereignty (First Peoples’ Cultural Council, 06/08/2026). The Tiered Sovereign Data Framework, endorsed in Resolution #2026-034 (PDF), offers one more way to set terms, and it obligates no Tribe. Evidence: Record.
  • Keep people at the center: Elders, Indigenous educators, and families carry what a chatbot cannot. Resolution #2026-051 supports policies that ensure AI “supplements rather than replaces” critical thinking, traditional learning, cultural education, language revitalization, Indigenous educators, Elders, and human relationships (Resolution #2026-051 (PDF)). Evidence: Record.
Tribal colleges and universities can set AI rules course by course and build the evidence that is missing.
  • Set AI rules course by course, with learning first: Navajo Technical University’s policy, as Tribal College Journal describes it, “leaves AI in the hands of the faculty, and its ethical use in the hands of students”: each instructor may allow AI, restrict it, or ban it in a course (Tribal College Journal, 11/08/2024). Evidence: Record, as reported.
  • Build the evidence Native education is missing: Small studies of AI and learning, run under Tribal research review with data held under the Nation’s governance, can answer questions no current study answers. The American Indian Higher Education Consortium (AIHEC) lists artificial intelligence among its cyberinfrastructure focus areas and hosts an Artificial Intelligence & Data Sovereignty Working Group (AIHEC). Evidence: Record.
  • Prepare teachers: Montana’s guidance names Tribal colleges among the partners that “may support joint professional learning, research, and educator preparation” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.
Families can ask the school four questions, and ask young people for the thinking.
  • Ask the school four questions: Which AI tools does my student use? Who reviewed them? What happens to my student’s words and work? How does the school check what my student can do without the tool? Montana’s guidance asks districts to include conversational AI in family communication “about approved tools, local expectations, and how to raise a concern” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record.
  • Ask for the thinking: Before and after any tool, ask a young person to explain an answer in their own words. How do you know? Who could we ask?
  • Talk about companion chatbots: Montana’s guidance is direct: AI tools “are not counseling, assessment, or crisis services” (Montana guidelines). Evidence: Record. Common Sense Media recommends that no one under 18 use AI companions (Common Sense Media). Evidence: Advocacy.
  • Keep family knowledge in the family: Stories, language, and teachings do not need to go into a chatbot. Before anyone types them in, read what the tool’s terms say it keeps.
Three questions for the AI Task Force, and one for counsel.

Resolution #2026-039 charges the task force to “propose a plan to engage in outreach and education efforts, and support Tribal consultation with federal, state, and local governments and AI companies” (Resolution #2026-039 (PDF)). Three questions for that work:

  1. How will the task force support the Youth Committee’s packet while youth lead it? Which help does the Committee want (research, review, printing, or outreach), who reviews drafts, and when does the packet go to member Tribes?
  2. Where should Tribal consultation on school AI begin? With the U.S. Department of Education leaving safety decisions to states and local schools, which state education agencies in the ATNI region should the task force approach first, and what should Tribes ask of them: tool review, data terms, the handling of Tribal content, or all three?
  3. What should any AI tool meet before it reaches the students of a member Tribe? Should the task force draft model terms on student data, language recordings, and cultural material, drawing on the CARE Principles and the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework, for each Nation to adapt or set aside?

Data Sovereignty

In Resolution #2026-034, member Tribes endorsed the Tiered Sovereign Data Framework (TSDF) as an ATNI-recognized standard for Indigenous Data Sovereignty and authorized its release as version 1.0. The framework sorts data into four tiers: T0 Open/Public, T1 Community Network, T2 Negotiated Partner, and T3 Sovereign Restricted. It defaults to the most protective tier, and only authorized human decision-makers may lower a classification. Automated systems may protect Indigenous Knowledge; they never expose it on their own.

The endorsement obligates no Tribe to adopt the framework; each Tribe makes its own sovereign determination about its use.

What the Resolution Names Directly

  • Indigenous Knowledge taken into training data without authorization
  • The reproduction of sacred or restricted Knowledge
  • Deficit-based framings
  • The reuse of Indigenous data beyond Indigenous governance

  1. Download the importable follow list for this task force (JSON). It holds the dockets and Regulation Identifier Numbers that Policy Sentinel can follow; the documents and bills above are for reading.

  3. Choose “Import followed list” and select the downloaded file.
  4. Press “Check for new” to look up each item.
